India: The Path to Development

ClassThu, Oct 22 08:30–10:00 (Boston time) · Global Markets · Carrie Elkins

Read the prep essay →

The Professor's Questions

  1. How does India’s past inform its future?
  2. Will India successfully reach its Viksit Bharat2047 goals? Why or why not?
  3. What is the relationship between democracy and economic growth?

Case Summary

The case opens with a scene from everyday life: in the Delhi heat of 2025, a white-collar worker with a fever can order food delivery, see a doctor remotely and have medicine brought to the door; a delivery worker who is just as sick finds the public clinic closed. This is the author's opening device for presenting India's development gap: digital services are already quite advanced, but stable jobs, basic healthcare and a decent life still don't reach most people. (p.1)

The case then takes us back to ancient times. India was once connected to the Mediterranean, Southeast Asia and China through monsoon trade, commercial networks, the spread of religion and the exchange of knowledge. After the Mughal Empire, the East India Company gradually turned from a trading participant into a tax collector and ruler, and the British colonial government then put taxation, order and resource extraction at the center of the administrative system. The case stresses that this period left behind damaged industries, inadequate basic education and public health, and an elite bureaucracy. Both ancient prosperity and colonial trauma later became important historical resources for how India understands its national identity and economic self-reliance. (pp.2–5)

Independence in 1947 came with Partition, mass migration and violence. The new state had to integrate the former British provinces and the princely states, and build a shared political order across religious, linguistic and caste differences. The 1950 constitution established a democratic republic; the first general election faced 176 million eligible voters, nearly 85% of them illiterate. India chose to establish universal political rights first, while keeping and reshaping the colonial administrative skeleton. The union and the states share power, with development tasks such as education, agriculture and health assigned to different levels. (pp.5–7; Exhibits 3–5)

The post-independence government drove industrialization through planning, state-owned enterprises and import substitution, and also carried out land reform and the Green Revolution and set up research institutions and elite engineering education. The licensing system, meanwhile, subjected investment, output and expansion to approvals. Food security and technical capability grew, but competition among firms was constrained, links between research, university teaching and industrial application were weak, and grassroots education and healthcare remained inadequate for a long time. From 1970, Operation Flood connected smallholder farmers to urban markets through dairy cooperatives. The 1975–1977 Emergency suspended civil liberties; when elections resumed, voters threw out the ruling party. (pp.7–9)

By 1991, foreign debt, an oil price shock and financing difficulties had produced a balance-of-payments crisis, with foreign exchange reserves enough to cover only a few weeks of imports. Prime Minister Rao and Finance Minister Manmohan Singh pushed through a rupee devaluation, opened up trade and foreign investment, and dismantled most industrial licensing restrictions. Afterwards, software and business process outsourcing grew rapidly on the back of global demand, and financial services and private companies expanded. But agricultural workers didn't necessarily have the language and technical skills needed to enter high-skilled services, and many moved into low-productivity informal work. The government also faced new capability requirements as it shifted from running businesses to regulating markets. (pp.9–11)

After the 2004 election, the Congress-led coalition government continued market reforms while expanding employment, education and health protections. The digital identity system Aadhaar launched in 2010, and in 2013 e-KYC lowered the cost of identity verification. After the BJP came to power in 2014, Jan Dhan bank accounts, mobile communications and UPI instant payments were further linked together, and digital infrastructure expanded into education, vaccination, health records, microcredit and e-commerce. The pandemic exposed the vulnerability of informal workers and public health on the one hand, and demonstrated the ability of digital systems to organize delivery at scale on the other. (pp.11–13; Exhibit 15)

The case ends by returning to Viksit Bharat 2047: becoming a developed, high-income country by the centenary of independence, with a vision of a US$30 trillion economy, manufacturing and exports, green growth and high-quality public services. At the same time, informal employment, weak foundational learning, low female participation, low R&D spending, political and social polarization, extreme heat and water stress all remain. Generative AI is also beginning to change the outsourced service jobs that once helped India grow. The case stops at a choice of development path: how to expand high-productivity employment so that technological strengths, state capacity and social inclusion reinforce one another. (pp.14–16)

Core Tensions

  1. Rapidly growing national aggregates vs. the productivity and living standards of the majority. US$30 trillion can be a macro milestone, but 88.3% informal employment reminds us that job quality and public services determine whether the goal is fully achieved.
  2. Peaks of technology and elite talent vs. widespread shortfalls in basic education, healthcare and employment. e-KYC can slash costs; it can't automatically turn a child who hasn't learned to read into an engineer.
  3. The speed of centralized decision-making vs. institutional correction and social inclusion. A strong mandate can push infrastructure forward; weak oversight can also prolong bad policies and damage long-term trust.
  4. Aspirations for self-reliance and security vs. competition and international connection. The colonial experience explains the appeal of self-sufficiency; 1991 showed the cost of closure and protection. Building self-reliant capability means avoiding a slide back into permanent protection.
  5. Existing strengths in services vs. catching up on manufacturing and broad-based employment. AI makes services more efficient but may also narrow entry-level jobs; manufacturing has job potential but is also constrained by automation, competition and logistics.
  6. A unified central vision vs. local delivery and climate differences. States differ in capacity and needs; constraints on heat, water and electricity directly affect labor, investment and fiscal returns.
