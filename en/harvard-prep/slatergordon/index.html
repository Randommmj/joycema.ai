Slater & Gordon IPO

ClassWed, Oct 21 13:30–15:00 (Boston time) · Finance · Martin Sinozich

Read the prep essay →

The Professor's Questions

  1. Analyze S&G’s “No Win—No Fee” business model. Does S&G’s consolidation strategy create value? Will it continue to create value as it grows?
  2. Does it make sense for growing entrepreneurial businesses to go public? What are the plusses and minuses? Should law firms and other professional service companies such as accounting firms be publicly owned?
  3. How much is S&G worth as a company? Use the financial model and assume WACC = 11%. Would you invest in this deal?

Case Summary

In 1935, William Slater and Hugh Gordon founded S&G in Melbourne, starting out with workers' compensation work for unions and working people. Years of representing individuals against larger institutions helped the firm build a consumer brand. As of the case date, the firm had nearly 400 staff, more than 140 lawyers and 21 offices across six states; total revenue in 2006 was about A$44.37 million. The headcount and office numbers in the text are as of the case date, while Exhibit 2's 2006 historical data show 114 professional legal staff FTE and 19 offices — the two should not be treated as the same year or the same basis. (pp.1–2; Exhibits 1–3)

The firm's core business splits into personal injury litigation and non-personal-injury legal services, at about 75% and 25% of core revenue respectively. The former relies heavily on No Win—No Fee: legal fees can be charged only if the case succeeds. The firm also runs project litigation/class actions, which can bring sizeable revenue and brand impact but irregular cash collection. An ordinary personal injury case usually takes 6–24 months, with a typical legal fee of about A$15,000. (pp.2–3)

While a case is under way, S&G pays for staff, offices, outside experts and so on, while recognising revenue based on the expected fee, stage of completion and probability of success, booked as a work-in-progress (WIP) asset. Exhibit 5 shows one successful case: A$4,250 spent in each of the first three periods, A$17,250 recovered in the fourth, for a final pre-tax profit of A$4,500. Book profit appears period by period, but cash keeps flowing out first. (p.3; Exhibit 5)

S&G's growth comes from client acquisition and M&A. Its telephone advice line receives about 30,000 calls a year, and its brand and referral network bring in clients. Since 2002 it has acquired and integrated eight small law firms; in the most recent year it acquired four, paying A$9.3 million, expected to contribute more than A$8 million of revenue in FY2008. Personal injury law becoming more complex has put pressure on smaller firms lacking systems and expertise, creating consolidation opportunities. (pp.3–4)

Growth needs capital — both to pay acquisition prices and to fund the outlays on new cases. The original shareholders could not or would not keep putting in enough capital; more debt would worsen the strain on cash flow and the shortage of collateral; and the business changes proposed by private equity investors were not accepted. As Australian rules changed at the time to allow outside ownership, S&G began planning an IPO, and its prospectus set out a clear order for handling conflicts: duty to the court first, then duty to clients, then the firm's other obligations and shareholders' interests. (pp.4–5)

The offer proposes to sell 35 million shares at A$1 each, comprising about 17.3 million sold by existing shareholders and about 17.7 million new shares. After the deal, fully diluted shares are about 107.78 million and net debt about A$5.4 million. Rosen must decide whether to participate, how to assess the growth, valuation and governance risks, and how much to buy. The case text gives differing dates for the listing timetable; this analysis uses only the decision point "before the 2007 IPO" and does not depend on the specific first trading day. (pp.1, 5–6; Exhibits 6–7)

Core Tensions

  1. A model value of about A$1.35 per share, versus dependence on long-term assumptions. The A$1 offer price is attractive, but terminal value makes up about 83%; a 24% margin cannot be treated as a law of nature.
  2. Growth opportunity, versus growth consuming cash first. More cases and more acquisitions both bring WIP and working-capital needs; projected cash flow is negative throughout 2009–2011.
  3. Expanding a professional services platform, versus rising integration difficulty. Brand and processes can be replicated; lawyers' judgement, local relationships and culture do not necessarily replicate in proportion.
  4. Liquidity for shareholders, versus growth capital for the firm. Not all of the A$35 million raised goes into the company; the use of funds has to be examined item by item.
  5. Outside capital's return requirements, versus courts/clients coming first. This constraint must be reflected in decision rights, incentives and disclosure, not just in the words of the prospectus.
