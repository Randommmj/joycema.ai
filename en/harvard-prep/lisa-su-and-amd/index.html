The Professor's Questions

  1. What were the strengths of AMD during its rise and into the 1990s and early 2000s?
  2. What were the root causes of AMD’s struggles?
  3. If you were Lisa Su, taking over AMD in 2014, what would be your first three steps to turn around the company?

My Answers

Q1. What were the strengths of AMD during its rise and into the 1990s and early 2000s?

AMD combined a credible alternative to Intel with real engineering strength and customer value. Its advantage changed as the company grew.

  1. A trusted second source. Customers wanted supply options. AMD could enter an established market by providing a reliable alternative. That gave it access to customers and the x86 ecosystem. [S1]
  2. Compatibility and value. Customers could use familiar software while gaining another price and performance choice. AMD64 later offered a path to 64-bit computing that protected existing software investments. [S2、S4]
  3. The ability to lead in selected technologies. Athlon and Opteron showed that AMD could develop important products of its own. This was more than a low-price position. [S3、S5]
  4. Partners that helped turn chips into usable systems. Support from computer makers and software companies mattered because customers buy a working system, not just a processor. [S5]
  5. A strong challenger identity. AMD's people-first tradition and engineering ambition could help attract commitment. I would treat this as a cultural asset, not proof that the company always executed well. [S16]

My conclusion: AMD's early opportunity came from offering choice; its stronger position came from making that choice technically valuable.

Q2. What were the root causes of AMD’s struggles?

AMD's struggles came from a mismatch between its ambitions, its resources, and its ability to deliver competitive products consistently. External pressure made that mismatch more costly.

  1. An uneven competitive position. Intel had greater scale and strong influence over the PC ecosystem. AMD also faced disputed commercial practices; the 2009 settlement shows that market access was a real issue. But external pressure cannot explain every product and investment decision. [S2、S15]
  2. Product choices that did not serve enough customer workloads well. The FX-8150 tests showed a weakness in single-thread performance. My management inference is that a promising design idea must be tested against how customers actually use the product. [S8]
  3. Large financial commitments reduced room for error. ATI added valuable graphics capabilities, but its acquisition also used cash and debt. A strategically useful asset can still be acquired with a financial burden that is hard to carry. [S6]
  4. The cost and complexity of manufacturing. AMD had to fund design and production while competing at a smaller scale. The foundry separation reduced direct ownership of fabs, but it did not remove supplier, process or delivery risks. [S2、S7]
  5. Too much to execute with limited resources. Weak PC conditions, several product lines and a need to rebuild CPU competitiveness competed for attention and funding. My diagnosis is that AMD needed sharper priorities and more reliable execution. This is an interpretation of its business position, not a documented claim that employees were unwilling to cooperate. [S9–S12]

The cycle I would worry about is: weaker products lead to pricing pressure and lost business; lower financial flexibility makes sustained investment harder; further delays then weaken customer confidence. This is a causal framework, not a claim that every link happened in every quarter.

Q3. If you were Lisa Su, taking over AMD in 2014, what would be your first three steps to turn around the company?

I would establish the facts and protect cash, choose a focused product path, and rebuild execution with customers and employees. These steps would overlap; I would not wait for one to finish before starting the next.

First, find out what can keep the company alive and what must be protected.

In the first month, I would review cash needs, debt maturities, inventory, supplier commitments and customer payments with the CFO. I would meet key customers and technical leaders to learn which promises were at risk and which capabilities were still strong. I would build a 13-week cash forecast and a longer product-funding plan. I would stop low-priority spending while protecting critical engineers and customer commitments. The cost is that some projects lose funding and some restructuring may be necessary. An equal percentage cut across every team would be a poor starting point.

Second, put the best people and funding behind a few connected priorities.

I would make high-performance computing the main direction, with a competitive CPU core and graphics as key capabilities. I would maintain useful semi-custom and embedded business to help support the transition, while judging each contract on cash, margin and engineering effort. I would review the CPU work already under way rather than pretend to start it from nothing. Projects outside the main direction would need clear customer evidence, a small budget and a stop rule. The cost is fewer options and greater dependence on a limited number of bets.

Third, make delivery credible again.

I would give each major product one accountable leader, connect engineering, sales and operations in a weekly review, and use shared milestones for performance, power, quality and delivery. I would ask people to report problems early and make it safe to challenge the plan. With key customers, I would agree on realistic validation and launch steps. With the board, I would agree on the funding and evidence needed across more than one product generation. The cost is that problems become visible and some public promises may need to be reduced.

By the end of the first 90 days, I would want three things: a funded survival plan, a clear list of product priorities and cancellations, and a delivery plan owned jointly by the teams and key customers. I would judge early progress by cash visibility, talent retention, engineering milestones and customer validation—not by expecting a new chip to appear in one quarter.

AMD's strength was that it offered a credible alternative to Intel, backed by real engineering. Its weakness was that it could not turn that strength into consistent product and financial performance. If I were Lisa Su in 2014, I would first understand the cash position and protect the people and projects that mattered most. Second, I would focus the roadmap on high-performance computing, while using valuable existing businesses to support the transition. Third, I would rebuild trust through clear ownership, honest reporting and realistic customer commitments. The difficult leadership choice is deciding what to stop, while keeping people committed to a plan whose results will take years to appear.

Case Summary

When AMD first entered the microprocessor market, it held an important position: a credible second source of supply for customers. The Computer History Museum's oral history with Intel insiders records IBM's requirement for a second supplier, and also AMD's reputation at the time as a reliable supplier. That let it enter a market that already had software, PC makers and users, without having to build all the demand on its own. [S1]

In the 1990s, AMD steadily strengthened its own processor design capabilities. After the K6, the Athlon, which began shipping in 1999, became an important product; in 2000 AMD launched an Athlon that reached 1 GHz. In 2003, Opteron brought 64-bit capability to x86 servers while keeping the ability to run existing 32-bit software. This let customers keep their software investments and upgrade their systems gradually. Ecosystem partners such as IBM and Microsoft provided product or software support when Opteron launched. [S2–S5]

AMD also faced bigger rivals, R&D and manufacturing businesses that required constant investment, and pressure to keep iterating its products. When it completed the ATI acquisition in 2006, the announced deal value was about US$5.4 billion, including about US$4.3 billion in cash, with a new US$2.5 billion term loan. The acquisition brought GPU, chipset and other capabilities, but also added financial and integration burdens. Afterwards, in 2009, AMD put its wafer manufacturing business into the newly formed GLOBALFOUNDRIES, gradually moving to a model that relied on outside manufacturing. [S6–S7]

The difficulties on the product side did not end with the manufacturing spin-off. The Bulldozer architecture reached the market in 2011; benchmarks of the FX-8150 at the time showed its single-threaded performance was uncompetitive in some common applications and games. This is evidence about a specific product and specific test scenarios, and can't be written up as "every AMD product lagged at every task." At the same time, weakness in the consumer PC market hurt the traditional business. [S8–S10]

The transformation had already begun before 2014. Jim Keller returned in 2012, reporting to CTO Mark Papermaster and leading CPU core design; Lisa Su joined AMD the same year. In 2013 Sony and Microsoft announced that their next-generation game consoles would use AMD semi-custom chips. In a 2017 interview, Lisa Su said directly that the new high-performance CPU core had been in development for about four years. So the new CPU roadmap, the team and the console business can't all be credited to the period after she became CEO in 2014. [S11–S14]

On October 8, 2014, Lisa Su succeeded Rory Read. Quarterly data from around the handover show a company with divergent business performance: the traditional Computing and Graphics segment was under pressure, while the Enterprise, Embedded and Semi-Custom segment was profitable. What she had to work out was how to get cash, talent, product direction and customer commitments all supporting the same long-term path. [S9–S10, S13]

Core Tensions

  1. Short-term survival and long-term R&D must both hold. Cash is limited, and developing a new core takes years; cutting spending evenly across the board might improve immediate outgoings but damage competitiveness in the next round.
  2. There are opportunities in several markets, but only one engineering team. CPU, GPU, consoles, embedded and other directions can't all get equal priority; focusing means giving up some potential revenue.
  3. Recognise the value left by predecessors while changing resource allocation. The manufacturing spin-off, the console customers and the CPU team already exist; the new CEO has to judge what to continue and what to scale back.
  4. The technology is worth investing in, but customers may not be willing to adopt it right away. Customers still need to validate software, quality, supply and the future roadmap; R&D and commercial trust have to be built together.
  5. A long-term direction needs patience; project management needs timely course correction. Sticking with a direction can't become an excuse for tolerating hidden problems or endless delays.
