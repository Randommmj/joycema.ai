The Professor's Questions

This case follows the entrepreneurial journey of Janan Knust, founder and CEO of KLog.co, a Chile-based logistics company that has grown from a traditional freight forwarder into a technology-enabled logistics platform serving over 1,500 clients across Latin America. In a highly fragmented and commoditized industry, KLog has defied structural limitations by combining a high-touch freight operation with a proprietary SaaS platform and a distinctive organizational culture defined by its AFIRE values: Alegría (Joy), Foco (Focus), Incomodidad (Discomfort), Resiliencia (Resilience), and Estudio (Study).

  1. Is the freight forwarding industry attractive? Why?
  2. What are the most innovative features of Klog’s business model? Is Klog solving the problems in the freight forwarding industry?
  3. What is Klog’s competitive advantage? Is it sustainable?
  4. Provide an assessment of the growth trajectory of Klog. What are its strengths? Its weaknesses?
  5. What growth strategy would you recommend Janan Knust to follow? Why?
  6. Should Klog finance the proposed growth strategy using external funds? Explain your answer.

Case Summary

December 2023, Santiago. Janan Knust, espresso in hand, stares at the mural by the headquarters entrance. It depicts KLog's eleven years so far, and employees are proud of it. He hopes the mural will have room for the next eleven years too. The company has grown from an analog freight forwarder into a digital platform providing both software and freight to more than 1,500 importers and exporters across Latin America: over 10,000 forwarding transactions a year, a SaaS product that reached US$500,000 in ARR just over a year after launch, consistently profitable, never externally funded. There are already several term sheets on his desk, with more to come. He and his team keep asking the same set of questions: keep growing on their own cash, or take their first outside money? How should resources be split between forwarding and SaaS? Will each decision damage "the layer that makes KLog KLog"?

Knust's path doesn't look like a freight forwarder's. Born in Israel, with dyslexia and ADHD, he was cut from the Universidad Católica football team at 17, went to Switzerland to study hotel management, and worked his way up from dishwasher to head of food and beverage at a global luxury hotel chain. He brought the hospitality industry's obsession with service and culture into an industry that "isn't customer-centric." In 2012, his father Werner, who ran fish-export logistics in Chile, asked him to join a meeting with Kerry Logistics. During the meeting he found forwarding simple; when his father turned down the Chinese company's proposal, he cut in: I'll represent Kerry in Latin America. That's how KLog began — initially just Kerry's agent in Chile. The two key early hires he kept — first salesperson Anthony Asp (later CCO) and first operations hire Marianela Catalán (later head of customer service in Chile) — are both still there.

The first crossroads was survival. The business was expanding, and the brand and relationships were there, but it didn't make money for the first three years, and its debt to Kerry piled up to the brink of bankruptcy. Knust flew to China and secured a line of credit. As he said later: good relationships aren't enough in a low-margin, highly commoditized industry; the new management principle had to be "who we are," not "what we do." He gradually replaced people from traditional industry backgrounds, brought in industry veteran Marcelo Delgado to run finance, and wrote resilience and passion into the culture. Once the company recovered, he began to feel that a mature industry was mature enough to be unbundled by technology.

The second crossroads was becoming a tech company. In 2017 KLog made a strategic investment in a local startup, whose CTO, Álvaro Serrano, stayed on and built the platform from scratch. It started as an internal TMS: purchase orders, instant quotes, sailing schedules, tracking, reports and centralized documents. Technology was a "nice to have" at the time — six developers, almost all in their first jobs, with no product, design or marketing. At the end of 2020 Serrano said he couldn't keep going. They wrote a plan to "make technology the core value proposition," kept the CTO, and made "the clash between analog DNA and tech DNA" the management team's top agenda item. Customers started voting with their feet. William Bencuya, purchasing and freight manager at a large Latin American home-goods retailer, said: we switched to KLog because the platform can adapt to our operations — with others, a single change takes forever, and their platforms are thin and rigid; the freight itself is good too, and they handled the volume during the pandemic. Felipe Gomez, CEO of a Chilean sporting-goods distributor, said: beyond the freight, what we like is being able to track, store documents, see historical costs, compare prices and talk to our account manager.

The third crossroads was whether to take money. The pandemic drove freight rates to unprecedented highs, and importers often didn't know where their goods were or when they'd arrive. KLog's tech investment proved right amid the chaos: new clients and volume came in, and on top of the rates, revenue soared. Platforms like Flexport and Nowports raised money at record valuations. Knust knew that revenue, gross margin and EBITDA had all been lifted by freight rates, and that the tide would go out; he also knew that he'd grown the company for ten years as sole owner with zero bank debt, and that raising money would mean changing both mindset and transparency. He strengthened the team: Igal Meirovich as head of finance and strategy, early tech advisor Gabriel Gurovich as CPO, and Fernando Varetto as COO; commercial offices opened in Peru, Bolivia and Mexico; and Isaac Cañas came over from Rappi to be CEO of Mexico.

By 2024, KLog saw itself as Latin America's leading technology company in international freight and integrated logistics. Four countries, 200+ people, annual sales of over US$40 million. Revenue had fallen from a pandemic peak of about US$80 million, but TEU grew at a CAGR of over 30% from 2020 to 2023. Knust was named an Outlier Entrepreneur by Endeavor two years running and was included in EY's 2024 World Entrepreneur awards. The product comes as a pair: freight forwarding by sea, air, road and rail, plus warehousing, insurance, customs brokerage, dangerous goods and local transport; and a platform grown out of the operations team's and clients' pain points — centralized documents (even for shipments not moved by KLog), remaining free container days, real-time tracking and alerts, reports, and dashboards for sales and inventory, integrated with clients' WMS, ERP and purchase orders. To get people using it, they created K4All: ship at least one load with KLog during the period and the platform is free. Software-only clients pay by the number of containers tracked, with unit prices falling as volume rises, plus an implementation fee. The platform also offers transparent quotes and three-click "Book & Pay," letting clients bypass sales and book directly — the information asymmetry forwarders live on, dismantled by KLog itself.

Clients range from large Chilean companies such as CasaIdeas and Arauco to Crocs, Tommy Hilfiger, Makita and Lee. Forwarding sales use the Sandler method — aggressive and focused; SaaS has entirely different sales cycles, decision-makers and contracts, and building a separate commercial team for it is a management priority. Churn is inherently high in forwarding. KLog cut overall churn from about 55% in 2017 to about 40% in 2023 while still growing its active client count; clients on the platform retained significantly better, and by mid-2023 platform users made up 26% of active clients. In Knust's words: clients who switch forwarders will also become SaaS clients, because they contribute profit and ship frequently; competitors are still moving freight with pen and paper, and the platform will make people stick. Internally, the same operating system is used by employees, complex manual work is being automated, and AI has begun reading documents and extracting data. He's already worried: if the company doesn't grow exponentially, employees will run out of work.

The culture is codified as AFIRE. Joy, focus, discomfort, resilience, study. Hiring is extremely selective and deliberately avoids people from the forwarding industry; at the same time, most of the C-level comes from a personal network of people he's known since he was three. The office feels like a Silicon Valley startup: nap areas, on-site massages, draft beer; an annual all-staff getaway, KFunFest; and underneath it, KPEOPLE (interest-free loans, the Semillero intern incubator), KLEARNING and KGIVING. Knust says: culture is by far the most important success factor.

Money has always been sufficient, and always carefully thought through. During the pandemic valuation bubble they met all kinds of VCs and received term sheets, but ultimately decided to keep rolling forward on excess cash. The fundraising process itself forced them to professionalize operations. Now, with an expected US$15 million in excess cash for 2024–2025, four things are on the table: geographic expansion at about US$20 million, the digital platform at about US$12 million, fintech at about US$11 million, and executive hires at about US$2 million — about US$45 million in total. The case ends at his desk: should he keep expanding into more countries? Double down on SaaS or on forwarding? How much engineering capacity should go to new products? How will employees feel about technology continuing to replace jobs? If he takes outside money, which of the things that got the company here would be put at risk — and does he even want to take it?

Who has decision rights: only Knust. Who puts up the money: to this day, him and the company's own cash. Who's opposed: there's no openly opposed insider, but the "analog vs. tech" culture clash was made the management team's top agenda item; Serrano once threatened to quit. Who's the external pressure: Kerry once was; now it's the funds on the table, plus DHL / K+N, which are already catching up on technology.

Core Tensions

  1. Make the platform sticky first, or expand the map first (where the professor's Q5 / Q6 land): the four-year growth menu totals $45M, but two-year cash is only $15M. Platform + team = $14M, just covered by cash; geography $20M + fintech $11M must be financed or dropped. Exhibit 10 says the stickiness lies in the platform; 26% penetration and roughly 21 paying SaaS clients say the platform is still a seedling. Geographic expansion is the DHL / K+N playbook and would replicate the 40% churn rate in Brazil.
  2. Take $50M @ $200M, or keep rolling on its own cash: the terms carry a 1.5× preference, 7-year redemption/sale, and veto over audit and compensation. A 5.2× revenue valuation rests almost entirely on forwarding and the story; SaaS alone isn't worth that price. Taking it would allow all four things at once, at the cost of culture and control; not taking it means prioritizing.
  3. Forwarding cash cow vs. SaaS option: 98%+ of revenue is still forwarding, and SaaS operating profit is roughly $180,000. The platform's real job today is reducing forwarding churn, not becoming a standalone software company. But forwarding revenue per shipment has just crashed from $7,819 to $2,764.
  4. Dismantling the information asymmetry it lives on: Book & Pay and fully transparent quotes attack the profit source of traditional forwarding. Short term, this hurts the sales intermediary; long term, it buys trust and scale.
  5. Can AFIRE scale with the company?: culture is written up as the number-one success factor; yet the C-level is mostly childhood friends, and technology is replacing employees' manual work. Fundraising, going multinational and continued automation will all touch this layer.
  6. Industry window: big forwarders are catching up on tech, and the high valuations of Flexport / Nowports have receded. "If we don't do it, someone else will" still holds, but "we must raise now at unicorn prices and pace" is no longer the 2021 refrain.
