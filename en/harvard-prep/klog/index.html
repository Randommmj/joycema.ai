The Professor's Questions

This case follows the entrepreneurial journey of Janan Knust, founder and CEO of KLog.co, a Chile-based logistics company that has grown from a traditional freight forwarder into a technology-enabled logistics platform serving over 1,500 clients across Latin America. In a highly fragmented and commoditized industry, KLog has defied structural limitations by combining a high-touch freight operation with a proprietary SaaS platform and a distinctive organizational culture defined by its AFIRE values: Alegría (Joy), Foco (Focus), Incomodidad (Discomfort), Resiliencia (Resilience), and Estudio (Study).

  1. Is the freight forwarding industry attractive? Why?
  2. What are the most innovative features of Klog’s business model? Is Klog solving the problems in the freight forwarding industry?
  3. What is Klog’s competitive advantage? Is it sustainable?
  4. Provide an assessment of the growth trajectory of Klog. What are its strengths? Its weaknesses?
  5. What growth strategy would you recommend Janan Knust to follow? Why?
  6. Should Klog finance the proposed growth strategy using external funds? Explain your answer.

My Answers

Q1. Is the freight forwarding industry attractive?

No. As an industry, freight forwarding is structurally ugly. It is a place you enter only if you can change the game.

Five facts from the case:

  1. No barriers, no loyalty, price is the product. Knust: "The entry barriers are basically zero; anyone can do this." Customers use different forwarders on different routes and pick the cheapest. Switching is easy. Services look the same, so the industry is treated as a commodity.
  2. Fragmented, and the giants still do not own it. The market is ~$176B in 2024, growing a dull ~4.1% to 2029. DHL is 6%, Kuehne + Nagel is 4%. Small players are 60%. The big two grow by buying local winners, not by inventing a better product.
  3. The P&L is thin and people-heavy. Exhibit 3: average COGS 82%, gross profit 18%, EBIT 5% (range 1–11%). Almost all trade documents are still paper. COVID showed what that means: Bills of Lading could not move, cargo sat.
  4. Rates, not skill, swing the top line. Exhibit 5: industry revenue +30% in 2021, +15% in 2022, then -11% in 2023 — a 41-point drop from the peak. KLog's own ticket size went from $7,819 to $2,764. That is not a strategy problem. That is the industry.
  5. Big shippers bypass you. Large enterprises already talk to the shipping lines. SMEs are the ones who need a forwarder — and they are the most price-sensitive.

The only attractive reading is as a disruption target. Paper, fax, no visibility, low trust: that is why Flexport once printed an $8B valuation and why KLog's software found a job. But "the industry is broken" is not the same as "the industry is a good business." A good strategy starts by admitting the first sentence.

Q2. Innovative features — is KLog solving the industry's problems?

The innovative piece is not "they built a TMS." Many people built a TMS. The innovative piece is that the same operating system runs the freight shop and the customer's desk — and it still works when the freight is not KLog's.

The features that actually change the game:

  1. A dual product with one OS. Freight (ocean, air, land, rail, plus warehouse, insurance, customs, dangerous goods, last mile) plus a platform built with the ops team, not by a separate software company. Knust: the two products "merge into one." Employees use what customers use. That is how you get labor efficiency and fewer errors, and how you stop the software from becoming a demo.
  2. Forwarder-agnostic system of record. Customers can add and track shipments handled by other forwarders. Documents live in one place even when KLog did not move the box. That is a very different job from "please use our booking tool." It is a bid to become the customer's memory.
  3. K4All as a wedge. One KLog shipment in a window, and the platform is free. Most of the 26% "platform users" are almost certainly here, not in the paid SaaS column. $500k ARR ÷ ~$24,000 per SaaS client ≈ 21 paying software accounts. The free layer is doing the retention work Exhibit 10 shows.
  4. They attacked their own information advantage. Traditional FF pricing lives on relationships, negotiation, and opacity. KLog put a rate engine on the table and a three-click Book & Pay that skips the sales team. That is not a feature. That is a choice to give away the old profit logic in exchange for trust and volume.
  5. Hospitality DNA plus AFIRE, and they hire outside the industry on purpose. The innovative "feature" customers feel is service in a business that is not customer-centered. Culture is written as an operating system, not a poster.

Is it solving the industry's problems? For a slice of their clients, yes. For the industry, not yet.

  • Visibility, documents, quotes, free days, internal dashboards: yes. Bencuya and Gomez say so in plain words.
  • Paper Bills of Lading across the whole trade lane: no. One firm cannot fix that.
  • Commodity pricing and churn: only partly. Platform users stay (~80–93%, 100% by year 7). Everyone else still looks like the industry (~20% retained by year 5–7). Blended churn is still ~40% in 2023.
  • Low margins: only partly. Their FF gross margin is ~22% vs the industry 18%, operating margin ~11% vs industry EBIT 5%. SaaS is 90% / 35% — and $500k. Large forwarders are already copying the tech. The case says hopes dimmed.

So: KLog is solving the customer's problem of blindness and clutter. It has not yet made freight forwarding a good industry. The 26% number is the honest scoreboard.

Q3. Competitive advantage — is it sustainable?

KLog's advantage is a freight shop, a software layer, and a culture that were grown together — not software, and not "better service" as a slogan. It is sustainable only if they finish the job they started: get far more than 26% of clients onto the platform, without breaking AFIRE.

What is actually hard to copy:

  1. Culture as the operating system. Knust calls culture "the single most important factor" in the company's success. AFIRE is specific: joy as a working climate, focus as anti-distraction, discomfort as the growth rule, resilience as the permission to be wrong, study as the ban on improvisation. They hire people who did not grow up in freight. That is a real filter. It is also the most fragile piece under capital and geography.
  2. Software written from live operations. Serrano stayed because "technology alone wasn't going to cut it." The platform was iterated with the logistics team. Customers say other tools are "too basic and rigid" and take forever to change. That learning sits in the people and the workflows, not in a feature list.
  3. Exhibit 10 is the moat, if they can spread it. Platform users stay. Non-platform users leave like the rest of the industry. A competitor can copy a screen. Copying seven years of "this is where my documents and my history live" is a different problem — but only for the 26% who already live there.
  4. Service from hospitality, in an industry that does not do hospitality. That is why a large retailer stayed through COVID volumes, and why Gomez talks about the account executive in the same breath as the tracker.

What is not a durable advantage on its own:

  • The TMS features (quote, track, POM, dashboards). Large FFs are integrating the same tools. Flexport and Nowports already ran this play at much larger valuations.
  • Price. Ticket size just collapsed 60%. Nobody wins a price war in a 5% EBIT industry.
  • Personal networks. "I have known most of my C-level since I was three" is an asset at 200 people. It is a risk at 2,000.

Sustainable? Conditionally, and not yet at full scale. The combination is hard to copy. The combination is also unfinished. A 40% blended churn rate means the advantage is still sitting in a minority of the book. Raise the wrong capital, open three countries before the platform travels, or let automation hollow out the "joy" side of AFIRE, and the advantage becomes a mural.

Q4. Growth trajectory — strengths and weaknesses

The honest trajectory is: volume and clients are compounding, revenue is not, and the software is still a seedling sitting on top of a cash cow that just halved.

Strengths

  1. They take share when prices fall. TEUs 30%+ CAGR 2020–2023; FCL 5,256 → 14,718 (41%). Transactions 5,695 → 13,834, +35% in the year the ticket fell 60%. 2024 budget $50M on 15,000 shipments is a recovery plan, not a moonshot — and it is still a share-gain story.
  2. They survived without a patron. Profitable, no outside equity, no bank debt. $15M of expected excess cash over two years is real strategic freedom. The Kerry near-death in year three is in the muscle memory.
  3. The retention split is the best number in the case. Exhibit 10 is a strategy slide, not an HR slide. Once the platform is the customer's system of record, seven-year retention goes to 100%. That is the only path out of a 40% churn industry.
  4. Unit economics already lean their way. FF 22% / 11% versus industry 18% / 5%. SaaS 90% / 35%. The direction of the mix is right.
  5. They professionalized before they had to. New C-level in 2021, offices in three more countries, Endeavor / EY recognition, a fundraising process they walked away from — and kept the learning.

Weaknesses

  1. The P&L is a rate chart. $78.1M → $38.8M. Anyone who reads only revenue will say the company shrank. Anyone who reads only 2021 will over-value it. $200M pre-money is the second reading.
  2. Churn is still industry-like for most of the book. 40% blended. 74% of active clients are not on the platform. Paid SaaS is ~21 accounts. The "tech company" sentence on page 6 is ahead of the mix.
  3. Geographic density is thin. Four countries, Chile-heavy, 200 people. AFIRE, beer on tap, and a founder who hires from kindergarten do not travel as a kit.
  4. The two cultures still clash. Serrano almost left in 2020. Tech is now eating workflows. Knust can already see a world where, without exponential growth, "employees will not have work to do." That is a strategy risk and a culture risk at the same time.
  5. The bench is intimate. Most C-level known since age three is speed in a bootstrap. It is a blind spot when the next hires are CMO, CRO, and a fintech credit function.
  6. SaaS go-to-market is unbuilt. They say so themselves: different timeline, different buyer, different contract. Freight sales are Sandler and aggressive. That muscle does not sell a $24k software implementation.

Net: a strong regional compounder with one unfinished transformation. The next two years decide whether they become a platform that happens to move boxes, or a nicer forwarder with a website.

Q5. What growth strategy?

Deepen the platform first. Do not pave Latin America, and do not build a bank, until more than half the clients live inside the software. Fund digital platform + SaaS sales ($12M) and the executive bench ($2M) from the $15M of cash. Sequence geography. Park fintech.

Why this order — four numbers:

  1. Exhibit 10 is the only escape from the industry. Non-platform clients leave. Platform clients stay. Spending $20M to stand up Brazil, Colombia, Panama, China, and Miami while 74% of today's book still behaves like a commodity forwarder is how you export a 40% churn rate. DHL and K+N can buy local winners. KLog should not copy their path until it has something those winners do not have.
  2. $12M + $2M = $14M, and cash is $15M. The two initiatives that actually compound the advantage fit inside the bootstrap. The two that look like "growth" do not.
  3. SaaS is not the business yet; it is the lock. ~21 paying accounts and $500k ARR do not justify a software-company strategy. They do justify a two-year push: move platform adoption from 26% toward 50%+, turn K4All from a free gift into a funnel, and build a sales motion that can talk to a different buyer. Every extra platform client is a freight client who stops shopping every route on price.
  4. Fintech is a third business. Trade credit, inventory finance, payments, insurance: $11M, and none of the current muscle is credit risk. They just lived through a 60% ticket collapse. Do not put a loan book on a rate cycle you do not control. Revisit with a partner after the data and the volume sit in one system.

How to grow geography anyway, later: enter with the software, not with a warehouse. Sell the platform to a Brazilian importer who still uses three local forwarders. Become their system of record first. Stand up KLog freight only where the platform has already made you the default. That is the opposite of "open five flags and hope AFIRE boards the plane."

The cost of this choice: they will look smaller than a funded competitor in São Paulo for a while. Some term sheets will go cold. Isaac Cañas-type hires in new countries wait. The cost of the other choice: they become a nicer regional DHL, on someone else's clock, before the 26% becomes 60%.

Q6. Should they finance with external funds?

No — not for this strategy, and not on this term sheet. The growth plan I want (platform + bench = $14M) fits inside the $15M of cash. The term sheet is built for a different company: a funded land-grab that has to justify $200M by year seven.

Read Exhibit 14 as a strategy document, not a compliment:

  • $50M at $200M pre / $250M post, 20% out. 5.2× 2023 revenue, 4.0× 2024 budget. SaaS at a generous 10× ARR is $5M. Investors are underwriting a freight book that just fell from $78M to $39M, plus a story. That is the 2021 Flexport tape, after the case itself said hopes dimmed.
  • 1.5× non-participating pref = $75M off the top. Common — Knust — does not see a dollar in a sale until that clears.
  • Redemption or a forced sale after year seven. This is not patient capital. It is a calendar. AFIRE's "discomfort" and a redemption clock are not the same kind of urgency.
  • One board seat with veto on audit and compensation. The first place culture gets rewritten is how people are paid and what gets measured. That veto sits exactly there.

They already took the useful part of fundraising once: the process professionalized the company, built relationships, and confirmed the story. They walked away. That was the right trade in the boom. It is still the right trade after the bust, if they are willing to sequence.

When would I say yes? Three conditions, none of which are in Exhibit 14: a smaller check that only funds a later geo push ($10–15M, not $50M); no redemption / no sale right on a seven-year fuse; a partner who brings Brazil or China operations, not just a multiple. Until then, outside money is not cheaper than the $15M they already have. It is more expensive in the one asset the case keeps naming: the thing that made KLog special.

The cost of "no": a slower flag count; a funded competitor who can outbid them on a few lanes; some pride. The cost of "yes" on this paper: they will be asked to become the company on the mural's next panel in someone else's handwriting.

Case Summary

December 2023, Santiago. Janan Knust, espresso in hand, stares at the mural by the headquarters entrance. It depicts KLog's eleven years so far, and employees are proud of it. He hopes the mural will have room for the next eleven years too. The company has grown from an analog freight forwarder into a digital platform providing both software and freight to more than 1,500 importers and exporters across Latin America: over 10,000 forwarding transactions a year, a SaaS product that reached US$500,000 in ARR just over a year after launch, consistently profitable, never externally funded. There are already several term sheets on his desk, with more to come. He and his team keep asking the same set of questions: keep growing on their own cash, or take their first outside money? How should resources be split between forwarding and SaaS? Will each decision damage "the layer that makes KLog KLog"?

Knust's path doesn't look like a freight forwarder's. Born in Israel, with dyslexia and ADHD, he was cut from the Universidad Católica football team at 17, went to Switzerland to study hotel management, and worked his way up from dishwasher to head of food and beverage at a global luxury hotel chain. He brought the hospitality industry's obsession with service and culture into an industry that "isn't customer-centric." In 2012, his father Werner, who ran fish-export logistics in Chile, asked him to join a meeting with Kerry Logistics. During the meeting he found forwarding simple; when his father turned down the Chinese company's proposal, he cut in: I'll represent Kerry in Latin America. That's how KLog began — initially just Kerry's agent in Chile. The two key early hires he kept — first salesperson Anthony Asp (later CCO) and first operations hire Marianela Catalán (later head of customer service in Chile) — are both still there.

The first crossroads was survival. The business was expanding, and the brand and relationships were there, but it didn't make money for the first three years, and its debt to Kerry piled up to the brink of bankruptcy. Knust flew to China and secured a line of credit. As he said later: good relationships aren't enough in a low-margin, highly commoditized industry; the new management principle had to be "who we are," not "what we do." He gradually replaced people from traditional industry backgrounds, brought in industry veteran Marcelo Delgado to run finance, and wrote resilience and passion into the culture. Once the company recovered, he began to feel that a mature industry was mature enough to be unbundled by technology.

The second crossroads was becoming a tech company. In 2017 KLog made a strategic investment in a local startup, whose CTO, Álvaro Serrano, stayed on and built the platform from scratch. It started as an internal TMS: purchase orders, instant quotes, sailing schedules, tracking, reports and centralized documents. Technology was a "nice to have" at the time — six developers, almost all in their first jobs, with no product, design or marketing. At the end of 2020 Serrano said he couldn't keep going. They wrote a plan to "make technology the core value proposition," kept the CTO, and made "the clash between analog DNA and tech DNA" the management team's top agenda item. Customers started voting with their feet. William Bencuya, purchasing and freight manager at a large Latin American home-goods retailer, said: we switched to KLog because the platform can adapt to our operations — with others, a single change takes forever, and their platforms are thin and rigid; the freight itself is good too, and they handled the volume during the pandemic. Felipe Gomez, CEO of a Chilean sporting-goods distributor, said: beyond the freight, what we like is being able to track, store documents, see historical costs, compare prices and talk to our account manager.

The third crossroads was whether to take money. The pandemic drove freight rates to unprecedented highs, and importers often didn't know where their goods were or when they'd arrive. KLog's tech investment proved right amid the chaos: new clients and volume came in, and on top of the rates, revenue soared. Platforms like Flexport and Nowports raised money at record valuations. Knust knew that revenue, gross margin and EBITDA had all been lifted by freight rates, and that the tide would go out; he also knew that he'd grown the company for ten years as sole owner with zero bank debt, and that raising money would mean changing both mindset and transparency. He strengthened the team: Igal Meirovich as head of finance and strategy, early tech advisor Gabriel Gurovich as CPO, and Fernando Varetto as COO; commercial offices opened in Peru, Bolivia and Mexico; and Isaac Cañas came over from Rappi to be CEO of Mexico.

By 2024, KLog saw itself as Latin America's leading technology company in international freight and integrated logistics. Four countries, 200+ people, annual sales of over US$40 million. Revenue had fallen from a pandemic peak of about US$80 million, but TEU grew at a CAGR of over 30% from 2020 to 2023. Knust was named an Outlier Entrepreneur by Endeavor two years running and was included in EY's 2024 World Entrepreneur awards. The product comes as a pair: freight forwarding by sea, air, road and rail, plus warehousing, insurance, customs brokerage, dangerous goods and local transport; and a platform grown out of the operations team's and clients' pain points — centralized documents (even for shipments not moved by KLog), remaining free container days, real-time tracking and alerts, reports, and dashboards for sales and inventory, integrated with clients' WMS, ERP and purchase orders. To get people using it, they created K4All: ship at least one load with KLog during the period and the platform is free. Software-only clients pay by the number of containers tracked, with unit prices falling as volume rises, plus an implementation fee. The platform also offers transparent quotes and three-click "Book & Pay," letting clients bypass sales and book directly — the information asymmetry forwarders live on, dismantled by KLog itself.

Clients range from large Chilean companies such as CasaIdeas and Arauco to Crocs, Tommy Hilfiger, Makita and Lee. Forwarding sales use the Sandler method — aggressive and focused; SaaS has entirely different sales cycles, decision-makers and contracts, and building a separate commercial team for it is a management priority. Churn is inherently high in forwarding. KLog cut overall churn from about 55% in 2017 to about 40% in 2023 while still growing its active client count; clients on the platform retained significantly better, and by mid-2023 platform users made up 26% of active clients. In Knust's words: clients who switch forwarders will also become SaaS clients, because they contribute profit and ship frequently; competitors are still moving freight with pen and paper, and the platform will make people stick. Internally, the same operating system is used by employees, complex manual work is being automated, and AI has begun reading documents and extracting data. He's already worried: if the company doesn't grow exponentially, employees will run out of work.

The culture is codified as AFIRE. Joy, focus, discomfort, resilience, study. Hiring is extremely selective and deliberately avoids people from the forwarding industry; at the same time, most of the C-level comes from a personal network of people he's known since he was three. The office feels like a Silicon Valley startup: nap areas, on-site massages, draft beer; an annual all-staff getaway, KFunFest; and underneath it, KPEOPLE (interest-free loans, the Semillero intern incubator), KLEARNING and KGIVING. Knust says: culture is by far the most important success factor.

Money has always been sufficient, and always carefully thought through. During the pandemic valuation bubble they met all kinds of VCs and received term sheets, but ultimately decided to keep rolling forward on excess cash. The fundraising process itself forced them to professionalize operations. Now, with an expected US$15 million in excess cash for 2024–2025, four things are on the table: geographic expansion at about US$20 million, the digital platform at about US$12 million, fintech at about US$11 million, and executive hires at about US$2 million — about US$45 million in total. The case ends at his desk: should he keep expanding into more countries? Double down on SaaS or on forwarding? How much engineering capacity should go to new products? How will employees feel about technology continuing to replace jobs? If he takes outside money, which of the things that got the company here would be put at risk — and does he even want to take it?

Core Tensions

  1. Make the platform sticky first, or expand the map first (where the professor's Q5 / Q6 land): the four-year growth menu totals $45M, but two-year cash is only $15M. Platform + team = $14M, just covered by cash; geography $20M + fintech $11M must be financed or dropped. Exhibit 10 says the stickiness lies in the platform; 26% penetration and roughly 21 paying SaaS clients say the platform is still a seedling. Geographic expansion is the DHL / K+N playbook and would replicate the 40% churn rate in Brazil.
  2. Take $50M @ $200M, or keep rolling on its own cash: the terms carry a 1.5× preference, 7-year redemption/sale, and veto over audit and compensation. A 5.2× revenue valuation rests almost entirely on forwarding and the story; SaaS alone isn't worth that price. Taking it would allow all four things at once, at the cost of culture and control; not taking it means prioritizing.
  3. Forwarding cash cow vs. SaaS option: 98%+ of revenue is still forwarding, and SaaS operating profit is roughly $180,000. The platform's real job today is reducing forwarding churn, not becoming a standalone software company. But forwarding revenue per shipment has just crashed from $7,819 to $2,764.
  4. Dismantling the information asymmetry it lives on: Book & Pay and fully transparent quotes attack the profit source of traditional forwarding. Short term, this hurts the sales intermediary; long term, it buys trust and scale.
  5. Can AFIRE scale with the company?: culture is written up as the number-one success factor; yet the C-level is mostly childhood friends, and technology is replacing employees' manual work. Fundraising, going multinational and continued automation will all touch this layer.
  6. Industry window: big forwarders are catching up on tech, and the high valuations of Flexport / Nowports have receded. "If we don't do it, someone else will" still holds, but "we must raise now at unicorn prices and pace" is no longer the 2021 refrain.
