The Professor's Questions

  1. What strikes you most about Ohtani's approach to goal setting?
  2. What enabled him to turn an audacious goal into sustained world-class performance?
  3. Ohtani tracked both performance metrics (e.g., lower-body strength, pitch volume) and character behaviors (e.g., respect umpires, clean his room). Why do you think he included both? What role, if any, do character and karma play in achieving elite performance?
  4. The Harada Method translates long-term ambition into structured daily practices. What are its greatest strengths? What are its potential limitations?
  5. If you were creating a Harada chart today, what would you put in the center square?

My Answers

Q1. What strikes you most about Ohtani's approach to goal setting?

What strikes me is the distance between the ambition and the daily action—and how deliberately he connects them. His chart moves from an elite draft ambition to strength, control, mental habits, and even how he treats other people. The eight support areas and 64 actions force him to ask what the goal requires today.

He also separates the result he wants from the kind of person and environment that can sustain the work. His father's notebook made reflection a habit before the more elaborate chart existed. The chart therefore builds on feedback rather than replacing it.

I would not treat the exact teenage age schedule as the key achievement. He changed the route, including staying in Japan and adapting after injury. The strength is serious commitment with a revisable path. The risk is reading his success backward and assuming every item caused it.

Q2. What enabled him to turn an audacious goal into sustained world-class performance?

Several conditions worked together. He had exceptional ability and years of practice, but also a feedback system: specific observations, written reflection, and repeated adjustments. Strength work and technical skills were connected to performance rather than pursued as isolated tasks.

The environment mattered. His father and high-school coach gave feedback and structure. The Fighters offered a credible path to pitch and hit, so he did not have to abandon one capability prematurely. Later teams provided opportunities and support. Choosing an environment that fits the goal is part of execution.

He sustained focus through daily routines and adapted to constraints. In 2024, he could not pitch while recovering from surgery and concentrated on hitting and baserunning. The 54 home runs show an exceptional outcome that year, but they do not prove the chart alone caused it. Talent, coaching, resources, teammates, health, and chance remain part of the explanation.

For my own work, I would copy the feedback cycle and environment design, not his training volume. High effort without recovery, prioritization, or evidence can become counterproductive.

Q3. Ohtani tracked both performance metrics (e.g., lower-body strength, pitch volume) and character behaviors (e.g., respect umpires, clean his room). Why do you think he included both? What role, if any, do character and karma play in achieving elite performance?

Performance metrics tell him whether his capabilities are improving. Character behaviors help shape the conditions in which improvement is possible. Respect for umpires can support emotional control; caring for equipment can reduce preventable disruption; gratitude and consideration can strengthen cooperation. These are plausible mechanisms, not causal estimates established by the case.

Some behaviors also have value even if they never improve a statistic. Treating others respectfully should not depend on whether it produces a win. “Karma” can be understood as a personal reminder to contribute, notice his surroundings, and act with care. I do not interpret the case as scientific evidence that good deeds cause favorable events.

I would track a few observable commitments and invite honest feedback, while avoiding moral scorekeeping. A lost game does not show poor character, and excellent results do not prove virtue. The important combination is technical standards plus a way of living and working that people can sustain together.

Q4. The Harada Method translates long-term ambition into structured daily practices. What are its greatest strengths? What are its potential limitations?

Its greatest strength is translation. A distant ambition becomes support areas, specific behaviors, and a basis for reflection. The chart exposes missing conditions: a goal may need technical skill, relationships, attention, and an environment, not just more hours. It also gives a coach a concrete starting point for feedback.

Its limits are false precision and overload. Sixty-four actions can compete for limited time. The chart may look complete while omitting a critical assumption. It may reward checking boxes instead of learning. Some goals depend heavily on other people or luck, and a teenage life schedule should not become a rigid contract with the future.

I would treat the chart as a living hypothesis. Use one center goal, identify the most important constraints, choose a small active set of actions each week, and review evidence monthly. Keep a stop-doing list and room for rest. Change the actions when conditions change, and revise the goal if its purpose no longer holds.

Finally, one celebrated athlete is not a controlled study. The framework can organize effort; it cannot promise elite performance or eliminate uncertainty.

Q5. If you were creating a Harada chart today, what would you put in the center square?

Provisional classroom draft, pending my own confirmation: “Over the next 12 months, turn my OPM learning into 12 well-documented decisions or practice experiments, and build a sustainable cycle of learning, action, and clear writing.” This is a proposed center goal based on the current learning task, not an established personal commitment.

I would prefer evidence of use over a volume target for articles. For each selected lesson, record the original idea, the situation where I tried it, the choice I made, what happened, and what I changed. A decision that overturns an attractive idea can count as useful learning. An article without any new understanding should not count merely because it was published.

The eight supporting areas could be source reading, problem selection, small experiments, reflection, feedback, writing, time boundaries, and integrity. I would start with one case and one modest action, then review after four weeks whether the target and workload make sense. The number 12 and the deadline are suggestions for discussion. Joyce should replace the center square if a different purpose matters more.

Case Summary

Shohei Ohtani was born in 1994 in Iwate Prefecture, Japan. His father had played in Japan's corporate baseball league, and his mother was a skilled badminton player. He tried many different sports as a child, and his father also coached him for a time. After practices and games, father and son exchanged little notebooks, recording both the good plays and the mistakes. The point was to help him understand his own actions in words, rather than just looking at whether he won or lost.

His high school coach, Hiroshi Sasaki, required players to write down their dreams and introduced the Harada Method. Through goals, purpose, analysis, plan and action, it turns a long-term aspiration into behaviors that can be practiced. Ohtani's chart centered on becoming a draft prospect coveted by multiple teams as a first-round pick, and branched out into eight supporting areas — body, control, pitch sharpness, mindset, velocity, breaking balls, character and luck — each with eight specific actions.

The chart included technical items such as strength, range of motion and pitching, but also gratitude, courtesy, respecting umpires, tidying his room, picking up trash and reading. So the goal was not only an athletic result; it was also about the kind of everyday life through which he wanted to become an athlete. The coach also stressed helping one another, which placed individual practice inside a supportive social environment.

He originally wanted to go straight to the US after high school, but later chose the Hokkaido Nippon-Ham Fighters, because the team offered a development path that let him keep both pitching and hitting. The design of his environment and opportunities helped him preserve a rare ability. After deciding to move to the US in 2017, he joined the Angels and later moved to the Dodgers. The case describes his elite performance, but also records injuries and outside turbulence.

In 2024, recovery from elbow surgery meant he could not pitch, so he shifted that season's focus to hitting and baserunning, and reached the goal of at least 50 home runs and 50 stolen bases in a single season. The exhibit shows 54 home runs and a .310 batting average that year; in 2025 it was 55 and .292. The case also mentions back-to-back World Series titles and four MVPs, but a team championship cannot be guaranteed by one person's habits alone.

What is most worth discussing about this method is that it helps bring a grand aspiration down to today, yet must also allow a person to adjust the path after injury, a changing environment, and growth in ability. His high-school list of age milestones was not achieved item by item as planned; later achievements should not be treated as proof that every early plan was right.

Core Tensions

  1. A high goal gives direction, yet the specific path must be open to revision. Injuries and changing environments change which actions fit best.
  2. Controllable behaviors help performance, but cannot fully control injuries, opponents, selection or team results.
  3. Structured record-keeping can deepen self-knowledge, but can also become a burden of filling in charts.
  4. Character has value in itself and should not be treated merely as a tool for winning.
  5. A success story is inspiring, but on its own cannot prove that the method is sufficient to produce the same achievement.
