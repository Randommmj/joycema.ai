Belden and Digital Transformation

ClassTue, Oct 13 09:00–10:30 (Boston time) · Market-Facing Functions · Das Narayandas

Read the prep essay →

The Professor's Questions

  1. What is the "melting iceberg" situation facing the IA division? What are the key changes affecting IA's product line, sales, competition, or other business activities?
  2. What is the status of the IA division in the overall business and financial structure of Belden? What are the implications for initiating and sustaining any changes at IA?
  3. Consider the ESD initiative. What has changed in Belden's marketing, sales, pricing, staffing, incentive, and account-management processes? What has not changed? Why?
  4. What should Chand do about the three decisions described at the end of the case?
  5. What lessons do you take away from this case about selling solutions? Managing growth and/versus profitability?

My Answers

Q1. The "melting iceberg"

The "iceberg" is IA's core business: selling hardware — cables, switches, connectors — to plant managers and buyers. It is still big and still profitable. The ground under it is melting from below.

What is changing

  • Hardware is becoming a commodity. Margins on OT devices are falling. Customers want products customized to their industry, which pushes suppliers into narrower niches.
  • IT and OT are merging (Industry 4.0). Value moves from "the wire" to "the data": monitor, diagnose, control with AI. The Industry 4.0 market is projected from about $102B (2020) to $337B (2028).
  • Competition comes from above. Cisco, IBM, later Google Cloud and Microsoft talk to the CIO, CTO and CEO about what the customer's data can do. Belden talks to the maintenance director about part numbers. Chand: Belden was being "relegated to a widget supplier." IT vendors want to own the whole data pipeline — Belden only owns pre-ingestion.
  • Wrong buyer. Product sales meet people with a parts list and a target price. The real budget and long-term decisions sit with the C-suite; those decisions are CapEx, not OpEx.
  • Growth was bought, not grown. 16 acquisitions since 2007; 15 brands. Acquired sales teams kept selling legacy brands; distributors kept brand-restricted rights. "One Belden solution" was structurally hard to sell.
  • Incentives pointed the wrong way. Cost-plus pricing; revenue-only quotas; profit did not factor into bonuses.

None of this shows up as a crisis in today's P&L. That is the point of the melting-iceberg metaphor (Kotter): by the time the numbers scream, the ice is already gone.

Q2. IA's place in Belden

Where IA stands

  • 2019: almost half of Belden revenue, 54% of EBITDA — the profit engine.
  • 2019 → 2022: revenue $1.0B → $1.4B; EBITDA beat budget by $84M (despite ~10% dip in 2020 from COVID).
  • ESD has been self-funded, mainly by a pandemic boom in SPMRO (small projects, maintenance, replacement, overhaul).
  • ESD itself is still tiny: 69 customers, $7.7M revenue / $16.3M bookings — under 1% of IA. Goal: half of IAS revenue by 2026.
  • New consultants alone: 7 × $230K + 51 × $190K ≈ $11M/year, already more than ESD revenue.

Implications for change

  1. Room to act, almost no room to fail. A hit to IA's core hits the whole company and the stock. Chand cannot "burn the boats."
  2. Old business pays for new. Product sales and the channel fund ESD — that is why pay still leans product and the channel cannot be casually upset. The cash cow sets the speed limit.
  3. The funding cushion is temporary. SPMRO fades; edge-to-cloud R&D bills are coming. The $26M decision is the first time ESD must compete for capital with hard ROI.
  4. Strong results are political capital. Use them now to lock in structure (single P&L, pay redesign) while numbers look good.
  5. Prove ESD in board language: bookings, pull-through product sales, solution-level EBITDA — not story alone.

Q3. ESD: what changed, what didn't, why

Area Changed Not changed (yet)
Organization 9 product P&Ls → 2 → 1; direct sales from region/product → vertical Channel managers' jobs stayed the same
Marketing CICs (Stuttgart, Santa Clara; more planned), use cases, VR demos Partner tiers (Elite/Premier/Pro/Authorized); brand-restricted distribution rights
Sales process 5 → 8 stages; MEDDICC; sell to C-suite EBIs; cycle 6+ months No formal handover between SAM / consultants / implementers
Pricing Value Calculator; paid network audit; pricing moved from product mgmt to sales; CapEx buyers Still one-time solution price (no recurring); products still cost-plus
Staffing SAMs; 7 digital automation consultants + 51 solution consultants; 6-week vertical training; new hiring funnel Talent still scarce (tech + industry); "Oh, the cable company" stigma
Incentives 70% revenue / 30% ESD bookings; credit at booking (not invoice) Still mostly revenue; profit not in quota; product KPIs unchanged; 50/50 planned but no date

Why not change the rest

  • Channel ≈ 70% of revenue and owns 5-day fulfillment Belden cannot replace. Leadership: no resources, infrastructure, or desire to replace partners.
  • If pay flipped to 50/50 overnight, reps lose income during a 6-month ramp and leave. Side effect already visible: SAMs hit product quota first, then ESD.
  • Pilot logic: start CPG, learn, expand. New partner category and handover rules wait for volume.
  • Brand rights came with acquisitions — contractual legacy.

Cost of half-change: every one of Chand's three end-of-case decisions is a symptom of what did not change (channel conflict, brand rights, revenue-heavy pay). People follow the pay plan, not the strategy speech.

Q4. What should Chand do about the three decisions?

Underneath all three: how does a channel built to sell parts absorb selling solutions? Turn exceptions into rules.

① NTE + French materials-handling / self-driving vehicle client — hybrid

  • Belden delivers the network audit and services (solution consultants / CIC). The client already expects to deal with Belden directly.
  • NTE keeps all product fulfillment at standard margin (it can meet the 5-day spare-parts window) + a finder's fee of ~5–10% of services, not 20% of $28K.
  • Do not bring in the German partner: punishes the lead source, adds language risk, teaches every distributor "bring us leads and we cut you out."
  • Codify as a lead-registration policy so this is not renegotiated deal by deal. $28K is small; the signal to 70% of revenue is not.

② City Transport Authority + Magenta — both, in order

  • Now: one-time exception for Magenta on this ESD deal only, labeled "ESD-validated project." Mass transit is IAS's largest serviceable market ($504M). Forcing a flagship customer across multiple distributors is a terrible first impression.
  • Do not make Magenta buy-and-resell from other Belden distributors (stacked margin, pushes Magenta to competitors).
  • Next: build the ESD partner category ($300K, ~6 months). Solutions cross brands by nature; this conflict will recur. $300K ≈ one ESD deal — cheap insurance that turns today's exception into a rule other distributors can see.

③ $26M — ~60% go-to-market / ~40% R&D, and set a pay date

  • Do not simply hire ~104 SAMs ($26M ÷ $250K) against today's 281 salespeople. Ramp ≈ 1 year; talent scarce; diminishing returns as accounts/verticals saturate.
  • Put a large share of GTM into consultants (the real bottleneck), placed near CICs and existing SAMs where synergies work.
  • R&D only for edge-to-cloud software tied to CIC-proven use cases. Window closing: Google Cloud launched a manufacturing unit May 2022.
  • Reading Table B: sales hire $2.7M EBITDA over 5 years vs R&D $2.15M — but sales figure ignores turnover; R&D ignores the 70% channel multiplier. Over ~10 years R&D usually wins. Mix is right.
  • Also: set a calendar date to move pay toward 50/50. Otherwise new hires will still sell cable first.
