The Professor's Questions

David Jacobs founded a high-end ski apparel company in 1978. In 1996, he sought external financing to support further growth of the company and structured a financial deal with CHB Capital Partners, a private equity firm in Denver that began in September, 1997. By 2004, Jacobs was ready to consider alternative types of equity transactions that would provide a source of liquidity to him and his family, including sale of Spyder to another apparel company and sale of a large block of stock to a private equity firm.

  1. How, if at all, does the value of Spyder depend on its ownership structure? What are the (other) primary determinants of value?
  2. Prepare estimates of the company’s value based on DCF and trading and transaction multiples presented in the case. How well do these estimates reflect the considerations for a sale that you believe are most important?
  3. Compare the alternative transactions on the last page of the case. Which one would you choose if you were David Jacobs? Which one would you choose if you were CHB? Who else is affected by your choices and how?

Case Summary

David Jacobs founded Spyder in Boulder, Colorado, in 1978. A Canadian downhill champion and national team coach, he had already crashed twice as an entrepreneur: Lange ski boots, after going public, recalled 25,000 pairs over their liners and was eventually sold; Jacobs Corp. sold 45% to venture capitalists and the rest to García, which went bankrupt in 1978. He turned down an offer from Hong Kong's Auyang family to stay on as a vice president and started two more companies of his own, one of which was Spyder: high-end racing sweaters, sold by mail order, made in Hong Kong, positioned with a "powerful, slightly menacing" logo.

The first twenty years are the story of a family workshop growing into a brand. For the first two years it ran out of the family kitchen, with his two teenage sons, Bill and Jake, helping mail out catalogues. To raise money, Jacobs sold stock to Hanson Industries in exchange for $75,000 in cash and a warehouse; when Hanson hit a financial crisis in 1982, he had to either buy it back for $50,000 or lose the company. Having no money of his own, he brought in his Osaka manufacturer, Tsunehisa Shimokubo: Shimokubo put in $50,000 for 50,000 shares, Jacobs took 25,000 shares, and they agreed he would get another 25,000 shares when sales reached $2.5M — he was convinced he had to own at least 50% of any business he was part of. The target was hit in 1986, and the two became 50/50 partners.

The brand claimed that "small spot at the top." Fabrics came from Japan and Switzerland, insulation from 3M, snaps from Italy, and together with suppliers it developed proprietary materials such as X-static, Thermaweb and Spylon. In 1988 it won the U.S. Ski Team sponsorship, and Picabo Street and Tommy Moe appeared at World Cups and Olympics in spiderweb-patterned race suits. Sales grew from $3.4M in 1987 to $7.9M in 1990, and the channel shifted from mail order to specialty ski shops. In Jacobs's words: the market wasn't big, but "if I can hold that small piece at the top, there aren't many people fighting me for it."

Jake represents another life running through this story. International business at the University of Colorado, top honours in fashion design at FIDM, a year at Ono Trading's factory in Japan, then managing production in Hong Kong, before Nike poached him to do apparel development in Singapore and Hong Kong. In 1995 his father called him back as vice president of design and manufacturing.

In 1995 the company hit a midlife crisis. Sales were stuck at $10–15M, Jacobs was nearly 65 and had always kept his money in the company; Tommy Hilfiger, Nautica, Prada and Armani had begun making ski fashion, and Hilfiger ran a two-page ski ad in Men's Health. Jacobs said: "This is my turf." His advisers concluded: either sell the whole company or find a minority partner — "selling 51% is the same as selling everything." In February 1997 he hired Robertson Stephens to take it to market, approaching both strategic and financial buyers; Nike was at one point rumoured to want a majority stake, but it went into skateboarding and hockey instead, and that was that.

CHB Capital Partners is a local Denver PE firm, and its name literally means Closely Held Businesses. Tad Kelly and John Flanigan (both Harvard MBAs), plus Blake Morris. Low volume, high touch: they look at hundreds of deals a year and do only 2–3; they don't buy whole companies but want to "partner" with the CEO; Kelly said equity capital had become as commoditised as debt, so "we have to offer more than money." Their sweet spot: revenue of $10–50M, stakes of 20–80%, holding periods of 3–5 years. They were willing to put in about $5M and take a minority stake, but required a minimum 30% return. After due diligence they felt the projections in the memorandum were high, and proposed a sliding stake based on cumulative 1998–2002 EBITDA: hit the memorandum's $20.5M and they would hold 35%; above $26.5M, it would fall to 25%; below $10.5M, it would rise to 60%. Jacobs rejected the sliding scale and insisted on a fixed percentage. The final deal: on September 25, 1997, CHB first invested $2.5M for convertible preferred stock, a 22% stake; by no later than the end of 1998 it could invest another $2M (of which $1M could have gone to Jacobs and Shimokubo as cash-out, and $1M to working capital). If both tranches were invested, the fully diluted stake would be 37.9%.

Once the money came in, the company was switched over to a different operating system. The board went from "we meet when I'm in Japan and toss a page into the minutes" to formal quarterly meetings, with Kelly and Flanigan joining the board and Morris watching day-to-day operations. CHB pushed them onto J.D. Edwards ERP — "If you want to be a $100M brand, don't install a $10M system, install a $400M one." It cost about $1M. In May 1998 they brought in John Walbrecht, 31–32 years old and from outside the industry (global head of sales and marketing at Dr. Martens, previously at Gloria Vanderbilt and Timberland), as vice president of global sales and marketing. They rolled out fixtures / concept shops, landing 1,500 square feet at Marshall Field in Chicago. Flanigan chaired the annual strategy meeting. At the end of 1998 CHB exercised the second $2M tranche; Jacobs and Shimokubo chose not to take the $1M in cash. Ownership became: CHB 37.9%, employee options 11.3%, Jacobs and Shimokubo 25.4% each.

Growth meant losing money first and making it later. Walbrecht took sales meetings, which under Jacobs had never cost more than $25,000, and turned them into a $150,000 Hollywood-style launch in Vail with 75 dealers invited; the industry's view of the company went from "sleepy little firm" to "player." He himself spent 250 days a year on the road, working out for retailers how to display the merchandise, what music customers should hear and what prices to charge. Blake Morris boiled the industry statistics down to a one-page chart: third in insulated jackets, being crushed by The North Face in shells — and only then did people react. Jake moved his father out of the design seat, expanded the product and buying teams the Nike way, and within 18 months was promoted to vice president of merchandising. Products expanded from tight race suits and the $300–$1,000 core line into Venom, Kyds, Stryke and a snowboard line, with prices reaching down to $100, and distribution into Sports Authority, Dick's and Macy's. In Europe they switched from multiple distributors to selling direct, starting in 2001 with Germany, Australia and Benelux, then France and Switzerland — more profitable on the books. In 2000 the books showed a loss of $972,000 and the bank got nervous; Kelly and Jacobs stripped out the one-off investments, the bank accepted two sets of numbers, "before investment / after investment," and the relationship was saved.

By spring 2004, the report card had turned a page. Net sales went from $27.3M in FY01 to $61.4M in FY04, and net income from $83K to $5.1M. The Canadian and Austrian national teams were added. Retailers valued the high margins and high sell-through; Spyder's principle was "always sell one fewer — one fewer than the market wants." Bill was also called back to run Northern California. CHB tried to exit in 2002, but the market wasn't there; by the end of 2003, this was the only unrealised investment left in one of CHB's funds, held for seven years, longer than the expected 3–5. Jacobs said there was a great deal of cash out there with nowhere to go — "smart money is looking for brands."

They hired two investment banks to split the fees and the responsibilities: Financo (retail) and Wachovia (consumer products, handled by Joe Pellegrini from the 1997 round). An IPO was ruled out: the company was relatively small and highly seasonal. The last page of the case lays out three workable structures: sell to a strategic buyer (an apparel company); sell a large block of equity to another PE firm; or Jacobs keeps control and CHB can only sell its minority stake to another PE firm. Jacobs was 70. He said that in recent years he had stepped back a little from the day-to-day, and that being fairly rewarded for decades of entrepreneurship mattered more to him than continuing to control the company's direction. But Jake and Walbrecht were "the company's creative engine," and an acquisition would rewrite their positions and those of the whole executive team. He had to balance himself, CHB, his two sons, and the company he had built, all at once.

Calculation notes:

  • 1997 implied equity value: $4.5M / 0.379 ≈ $11.9M (about 1.1× sales at the time).
  • FY04 net cash: cash $7,975K − other liabilities $3,814K ≈ $4.2M (fiscal year-end is the off-season; the $23.4M debt peak doesn't fall on March 31). Equity value ≈ EV + $4M; for class purposes, equity ≈ EV is a fair approximation.
  • CHB at $100M equity value: 0.379 × 100 = $37.9M / $4.5M = 8.42×; 8.42^(1/7) − 1 ≈ 36.5% IRR. At $70M equity value: 0.379 × 70 = $26.5M / $4.5M = 5.9×, IRR ≈ 28.9%, right up against their 30% floor.
  • Jacobs at $100M equity value: 0.254 × 100 = $25.4M in cash (if he exits fully).
  • Management's projected 2004–08 sales CAGR: 165.7 / 61.4 = 2.70, 2.70^(1/4) − 1 ≈ 28.2% — sustaining high growth after the year that "just took off" within an already very high seven-year historical 29.6% is exactly where a buyer will cut.

Who holds decision rights: Jacobs + Shimokubo together hold 50.8%, plus wherever the option pool leans; CHB's 37.9% cannot sell the company on its own. Who put up money: CHB $4.5M, Shimokubo $50K in 1982, Jacobs his entire career. Who is racing the clock: CHB (the fund clock). Where the external pressure comes from: strategic buyers' appetite for synergies, channel leakage, fashion brands like Hilfiger.

Core Tensions

  1. The liquidity / family continuity Jacobs wants and the clean, high-priced exit CHB wants are not the same transaction. A 100% strategic sale usually commands the highest multiple (Exhibit 9 strategic median 10.3× vs. financial 9.1×), and is also the most likely to buy Jake / Walbrecht and the brand's independence along with it. A large-block sale to a financial buyer can keep the engine, give Jacobs his cash, and let CHB exit too, but the price is usually a notch lower. Jacobs keeping control and letting CHB sell only its 37.9% means a minority discount — CHB's least favourite option, but the one that best protects family control, which is exactly what the 70-year-old Jacobs says he no longer cares that much about. This is where the professor's Q3 lands.
  2. The company's value on paper and the company's people are not the same asset. The Exhibit 5 projections assume the engine is still there, the channels are still clean, and Jacobs is still the "conductor / coach." Under a new owner, all three can change. Half of the gap between a DCF of $170–200M and LTM multiples of $70–110M is this assumption.
  3. Holding the small spot at the top and growing into a $100M brand have always been hurting each other. Getting into Macy's, building lower-priced lines and expanding snowboarding are where growth comes from; half of Morris's discussion is "don't damage this or that part of the brand." The wrong next shareholder will pick the wrong side.
  4. Jacobs's life lesson is "never below 50%," and now, for the first time, he says cashing out matters more than control. Lange, García, Hanson — three times he lost his grip; in 1982 and 1997, twice he wrote control into a contract. This time, at 70, if he picks the wrong structure, he will write the third loss into his son's story.
  5. The window is external: in 2004, PE dry powder and multiples are recovering, but CHB's fund is overdue. In 2002 they wanted to leave and couldn't. Now buyers are more eager than sellers — but on the sell side, CHB is more eager than Jacobs.
