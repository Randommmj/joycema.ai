Note on the Starbucks Case We will not be discussing the Starbucks case in class. If you have a few minutes, please skim the case text and Exhibit 1. You do not need to prepare the case or discuss it in your living groups, though I would be delighted if you did.

Case Summary

According to the case, Starbucks Korea launched a promotion for large-capacity stainless steel tumblers on May 18, 2026, using the name "Tank Day" and an onomatopoeic Korean slogan. To the team, these words seemed to convey capacity, sturdiness and memorability; to many consumers, the date and the words connected to historical trauma in Korea.

May 18 is linked to the commemoration of the Gwangju Democratization Movement; the slogan was in turn associated with official cover-up rhetoric from another historical incident. The problem came from the combination of words, date, region and public memory — it can't be caught just by checking whether a single line of copy is grammatical or obviously offensive on its own.

The case cites reports and company statements saying the team consulted AI tools while drafting the copy, that the approval chain involved seven managers, and that some approvers reportedly did not open the attachment. The materials do not provide the full prompts, model versions or each person's actions, so we cannot conclude that AI independently created all the content, nor precisely allocate each individual's responsibility.

The campaign was pulled within hours, but screenshots had already spread. The case describes apologies, management changes, boycotts and investigations, followed by nationwide early store closures for training on historical and social sensitivity. The fallout from a headquarters marketing mistake also landed on store employees. Training can fill knowledge gaps, but it does not automatically change who needs to see what, who can stop something, and who bears final responsibility.

Exhibit 1 places AI uses across twelve marketing-mix elements, covering activities such as demand insight, product, pricing, brand, channels, sales, advertising, promotion, packaging, display, service and fulfillment. So the case is not only about writing copy, but about how AI takes part in the entire market-facing decision chain. As generation gets faster, human judgment and organizational design matter no less.

Core Tensions

  1. The tension between more creativity and faster launches, and enough time for contextual understanding and checking.
  2. More layers of approval do not necessarily mean more effective judgment; responsibility can also get diluted layer by layer.
  3. The relationship between a global brand's consistent baseline and local history, language and social memory.
  4. The visibility of after-the-fact training versus the durability of changes to everyday processes.
  5. Short-term customer-experience metrics versus trust and respect, which are hard to quantify quickly.

My Answers

Q1. Was this mainly an AI failure or a management failure?

It was a failure of an AI-assisted organizational workflow. The tool may have generated or helped refine inappropriate material, but people selected it, paired it with a date, approved it, and released it. The case does not provide enough records to isolate each contribution. Calling it only an AI failure would hide the decisions that allowed the output to become a public campaign.

The failure was contextual. A product name, sound, date, and local history jointly changed the meaning. A generic grammar check or a second model asked whether the copy was “good” would not reliably test that combination. A reviewer needed the complete campaign, the intended market, and the release calendar.

Seven names in a chain do not prove seven meaningful reviews. If everyone assumes someone else checked the issue, another approval can add delay without protection. I would ask what each approver had to inspect, whether the exact final asset was visible, and whether anyone could stop the launch without penalty.

Training can improve knowledge, but it should support a redesigned workflow. I would assign a campaign owner, specify cultural review triggers, require local review for sensitive material, and record approval of the final version. I would also protect staff who raise uncertainty. These are proposals; the case does not show that such changes have already been implemented or proved effective.

Q2. Where can AI help across the customer journey, and where is judgment needed?

Exhibit 1 is a map of possible applications, not a claim that every application should be automated. I would group the twelve elements by the decisions they support and the cost of error. One model can contribute to several stages, but responsibility stays with the relevant business owner.

At discovery, AI can organize reviews, service conversations, and demand signals. Humans must check whether the sample represents customers and whether a pattern supports a causal claim. At design, it can propose products, positioning, packaging, and prices. Managers must test feasibility, actual benefits, and customer understanding.

At acquisition and purchase, it can draft campaigns, prioritize leads, personalize offers, and improve product discovery. Review must cover context, factual claims, authorized prices and terms, and whether the firm can fulfill its promise. A high click rate does not establish a good customer outcome.

At delivery and service, it can help forecast stock, retrieve policy, draft replies, and route cases. Decisions involving exceptions, conflicting policies, or distress need a clear human path. Finally, analytics should connect conversion to returns, complaints, retention, and delivery quality. Begin with a narrow use case and a measured baseline; expand when quality and net benefit are demonstrated.

Q3. What would you change immediately, and how would you know it works?

Immediately, stop the offending assets across channels, preserve records for review, explain the error accurately, and support front-line staff. Engage affected groups with listening and a concrete repair process. Do not imply that store employees created the headquarters campaign. Do not claim that lack of intent cancels impact.

Before the next campaign, require the owner to review a one-page release packet: final wording and visuals, date, audience, geography, evidence for claims, and any sensitive references. Add an independent local reviewer when a trigger is met. Give that reviewer stop authority and a rapid escalation path. Avoid adding seven generic signatures to every routine item.

Over the next month, pilot this process on a limited campaign set. Keep useful AI support for drafting and organizing information. Log model-assisted work and material edits enough to reconstruct the decision, without creating unnecessary data collection. Maintain a versioned calendar and reference set, but do not assume a sensitive-date list can replace local judgment.

Measure serious errors escaping review, meaningful issues caught before launch, review time, rework, complaints, and recovery outcomes. A rise in near-miss reports may initially reflect better reporting rather than worse behavior. Review samples independently and adjust thresholds. The cost is reviewer capacity and slower approval of some campaigns; prioritize it where harm is hard to reverse.
