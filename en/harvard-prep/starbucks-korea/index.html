Note on the Starbucks Case We will not be discussing the Starbucks case in class. If you have a few minutes, please skim the case text and Exhibit 1. You do not need to prepare the case or discuss it in your living groups, though I would be delighted if you did.

Case Summary

According to the case, Starbucks Korea launched a promotion for large-capacity stainless steel tumblers on May 18, 2026, using the name "Tank Day" and an onomatopoeic Korean slogan. To the team, these words seemed to convey capacity, sturdiness and memorability; to many consumers, the date and the words connected to historical trauma in Korea.

May 18 is linked to the commemoration of the Gwangju Democratization Movement; the slogan was in turn associated with official cover-up rhetoric from another historical incident. The problem came from the combination of words, date, region and public memory — it can't be caught just by checking whether a single line of copy is grammatical or obviously offensive on its own.

The case cites reports and company statements saying the team consulted AI tools while drafting the copy, that the approval chain involved seven managers, and that some approvers reportedly did not open the attachment. The materials do not provide the full prompts, model versions or each person's actions, so we cannot conclude that AI independently created all the content, nor precisely allocate each individual's responsibility.

The campaign was pulled within hours, but screenshots had already spread. The case describes apologies, management changes, boycotts and investigations, followed by nationwide early store closures for training on historical and social sensitivity. The fallout from a headquarters marketing mistake also landed on store employees. Training can fill knowledge gaps, but it does not automatically change who needs to see what, who can stop something, and who bears final responsibility.

Exhibit 1 places AI uses across twelve marketing-mix elements, covering activities such as demand insight, product, pricing, brand, channels, sales, advertising, promotion, packaging, display, service and fulfillment. So the case is not only about writing copy, but about how AI takes part in the entire market-facing decision chain. As generation gets faster, human judgment and organizational design matter no less.

Key People

Actor Role Relationship to resolve
Marketing creative team Used AI, selected candidate content Responsibility between generated suggestions and the decision to publish
Approvers/management Review and launch authorization Whether a sign-off corresponds to clearly defined review content and real time spent
Local operating company/Shinsegae Operations and public response Local historical knowledge, release process, store support
Global brand owner Licensed brand and shared reputation How global minimum standards and local judgment fit together
Store employees Face customers and public emotion Should not take the blame for headquarters' decisions; should get facts and escalation support
Affected groups and consumers Experience and judge the brand's behavior Repair should listen to their experience, not just explain that offense was unintended
AI tools Provide candidates and assist with analysis Hold no organizational authority and do not bear the brand's final commitments

Core Tensions

  1. The tension between more creativity and faster launches, and enough time for contextual understanding and checking.
  2. More layers of approval do not necessarily mean more effective judgment; responsibility can also get diluted layer by layer.
  3. The relationship between a global brand's consistent baseline and local history, language and social memory.
  4. The visibility of after-the-fact training versus the durability of changes to everyday processes.
  5. Short-term customer-experience metrics versus trust and respect, which are hard to quantify quickly.
