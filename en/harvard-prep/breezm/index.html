The Professor's Questions

  2. Does Breezm have a competitive advantage? If so, what is it, and how sustainable is it?
  3. What are Breezm’s key internal bottlenecks or constraints for growth?
  4. Evaluate the three growth strategies Breezm faces: operational optimization in Seoul, domestic expansion within Korea, and international entry. What are the trade-offs associated with each option?
  5. What should Breezm do next? Why? What are the assumptions behind your recommendation? How would you test those assumptions?

My Answers

Q1. What is Breezm’s value proposition, and how is it different from incumbents like Luxottica or newer players like Warby Parker?

Breezm sells a better fit, not just a printed frame. It combines facial measurements, an eye exam, frame adjustment, custom production, and aftercare. A customer should feel less pressure, less slipping, and better lens alignment. The product is the whole service, including a 45-day redo or return policy.

Luxottica combines brands, lenses, manufacturing, and retail at scale. Breezm also integrates activities, but uses that control to solve an underserved fit problem. Warby Parker initially challenged price and shopping convenience with $95 prescription frames and then built stores and labs. Breezm is not simply another cheaper online brand: its Korean model relies on licensed opticians and in-person consultation.

The trade-off is time and cost. Customers wait 10–14 days and staff spend substantial time on consultation. I would target people with persistent fitting problems first, rather than claim every buyer values customization equally.

Q2. Does Breezm have a competitive advantage? If so, what is it, and how sustainable is it?

Yes, it has an emerging advantage, but not a proven permanent moat. The strongest asset is the linked system: fitting data, optician software, manufacturing know-how, and customer feedback. Digital Fitting reduced a designer's 1–2 hour task to roughly ten minutes for a trained optician. That is a concrete improvement in the economics of customization.

The 31% cumulative repeat purchase rate and 12.5 inventory turns support customer acceptance and a leaner stock model. Neither proves exceptional profitability: 2022 operating loss was $2.901 million on $4.448 million revenue. Nor does collecting 1,200 data points automatically create a network effect. More data matter only if they improve fit, reduce remakes, or make staff more productive.

Equipment and many suppliers are accessible to rivals. Patented hinges protect some designs, not the entire business. I would defend the advantage through measured fit outcomes, faster learning, reliable optician training, and ownership of the customer relationship. It is strongest where these activities reinforce each other.

Q3. What are Breezm’s key internal bottlenecks or constraints for growth?

The immediate constraint is profitable throughput across the whole system. Printing capacity exists, but more output helps only when stores can attract, fit, and retain customers at a sound contribution margin. Eight stores and 15,000 annual pairs imply a simple average of 1,875 pairs per store, but store opening dates and mix must be checked before calling any location inefficient.

There are four internal bottlenecks. First, consultation and optician skills can limit daily capacity; customers spend about 90% of store time in consultation. Second, the two manufacturing processes need different quality controls, including dye consistency and finishing. Third, broad in-house functions create a high fixed expense base. SG&A alone was slightly above annual revenue in 2022. Fourth, the founders must manage retail, software, design, factories, and expansion at once.

The old $313 cost table includes R&D and SG&A, not just manufacturing. I would rebuild current economics by product, store cohort, and customer channel before deciding that scale alone solves the loss. Missing data include remake rates, customer acquisition cost, mature-store contribution, cash runway, and unused capacity by material.

Q4. Evaluate the three growth strategies Breezm faces: operational optimization in Seoul, domestic expansion within Korea, and international entry. What are the trade-offs associated with each option?

Seoul optimization offers the lowest learning cost. Existing stores can test appointments, consultation design, optician training, product mix, and factory utilization. Its weakness is a ceiling: Korean prices are highly competitive, and many customers may see less need for customization. Optimization must have a deadline and clear targets.

Domestic expansion tests whether the model can travel without adding another country. Regional cities can fill existing factories and broaden demand. But new stores still require licensed partners, training, marketing, and cash. It may replicate weak economics, and a small market share does not mean every new location is attractive.

International entry offers larger markets and potentially stronger willingness to pay for fit. The US market was about $42 billion in the case, compared with Korea's $2 billion. But market size is not accessible demand. Local service, prescriptions, returns, distribution, regulation, and acquisition costs must be validated. I would use a tightly bounded pilot rather than a multi-country launch.

My ranking is Seoul economics first, a small domestic replication test second, then an overseas pilot when evidence supports it. This sequence has an opportunity cost: rivals may move sooner. I would still run low-cost overseas demand interviews in parallel, without committing a full store network.

Q5. What should Breezm do next? Why? What are the assumptions behind your recommendation? How would you test those assumptions?

I would authorize a 90-day operating proof in Seoul and reserve a small budget for overseas customer discovery. The operating work would measure contribution after actual service labor, remake cost, and customer acquisition cost; it would compare mature stores and products rather than only group gross margin. I would also test whether better scheduling and digital fitting increase completed, satisfactory orders per optician hour.

My assumptions are that demand can grow without a damaging price cut, fit remains consistently better as volume rises, existing factories can absorb more orders, and current cash can fund the test. I would test price with limited offers and real bookings, quality with 45-day return and remake outcomes, capacity with controlled batches, and demand with repeatable acquisition cohorts. Any numerical success thresholds should be approved after baseline measurement; they are not supplied by the case.

If mature stores show positive contribution after all local costs, and a second location can reproduce it without founder intervention, open one domestic test store. If interviews and paid commitments show that an overseas niche offers enough extra contribution to cover service and acquisition costs, test one city with a qualified local partner and legal review. Stop or redesign if remakes rise or acquisition costs consume the extra margin.

I would not make the 2027 IPO date the decision rule. The price of this choice is slower headline growth and possible delay of the IPO. The benefit is learning whether a customized product can support a repeatable business before multiplying its fixed commitments.

Case Summary

Park had previously built the eyewear brand ALO and brought experience of the industry, consumers and retail; Sung moved from accounting and investment banking into manufacturing entrepreneurship, and saw how well small, high-value eyewear suited 3D printing. The two met in 2016 and, drawing on Humax Optics' manufacturing capabilities, spent a year on research before founding Breezm in 2017. Its point of entry was the problem that traditional standard frames don't properly fit every face.

In 2018 the company tested demand with ten pop-up stores, selling 25 pairs on the first day. At the end of the year it opened its first permanent store. It linked face scanning, style recommendations, eye exams, digital fitting, made-to-order production, and a re-check at pickup; customers were buying comfort and optical fit, with 3D printing as the means of delivery. In 2019 it introduced Face Ruler and AI Fit Finder; in 2021 its digital fitting system turned work that used to take a designer 1–2 hours into roughly ten minutes for an ordinary optometrist after one day of training.

The company kept expanding its polymer styles and in 2022 launched a titanium line. Metal 3D printing was too costly, so the team switched to laser cutting and proprietary hinges. This shows that its core promise is custom fit; not every product has to use the same manufacturing technology. Design, R&D, the factory and in-store service are run by the company, while lenses are bought from outside suppliers.

By 2022, Breezm had eight stores and annual sales of about 15,000 pairs. Revenue grew from US$1.7 million in 2020 to US$4.448 million in 2022 (exhibit basis), with gross margin of about 60.5%; but operating losses widened from US$1.64 million to US$2.901 million. The factory had idle capacity, stores were built around one-on-one consultations, about 90% of customers' time in store went to consultation, and delivery took 10–14 days.

The two founders hope to break even in 2024 and go public in 2027, and face three paths: first improve the efficiency of existing operations in Seoul, replicate stores in other Korean cities, or enter larger overseas markets with different pricing environments. Korea accounts for only about 1.4% of the global market, but overseas opportunities come with the costs of brand-building and customer acquisition, local service and regulatory adaptation. The case stops at the moment of choosing the sequence of growth.

Core Tensions

  1. Prove the existing model can make money first, or grab overseas demand better suited to customisation. Optimising first costs speed; going abroad first may replicate the losses.
  2. Made-to-order manufacturing reduces inventory, but doesn't eliminate consultation, quality and after-sales costs.
  3. Lower prices can expand demand, but squeeze the unit contribution that covers fixed capacity.
  4. Controlling the whole chain improves fit, but also loads management, cash and talent constraints onto a startup all at once.
  5. The IPO timeline drives scale expansion, but can't substitute for genuine validation of unit economics.
