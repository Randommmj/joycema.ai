The Professor's Questions

  1. How serious were Stephen Richards’s actions?
  2. If Computer Associates achieved the same financial results through GAAP flexibility, does your answer to Question 1 change?
  3. Suppose you were placed in Stephen Richards’s position at Computer Associates and were under pressure to extend the fiscal quarter. How would you have handled the situation differently? What would be the expected consequences of those actions?

Case Summary

Computer Associates (CA) was founded in 1976 in enterprise software and grew into a large software company through acquisitions and global expansion. Stephen Richards joined the Sydney office in 1988, rose quickly, and became head of worldwide sales in 2000. The sales organization revolved around quarterly targets: strong results brought large bonuses and stock options, while missing targets brought pressure.

Customers typically bought three- to ten-year licences. Under the accounting treatment of the time as described in the case, once a contract was signed, the software delivered and collection reasonably assured, the present value of the licence fee could be recognised in that quarter; usually at least 80% of the fee was allocated to the licence. So the signing date of a few large contracts could significantly change a quarter's revenue. Customers knew they had negotiating leverage at quarter-end and often pushed signing to the last minute.

Some managers effectively extended the fiscal quarter, booking contracts signed after quarter-end into the previous quarter. The products, contracts and cash could be real, but the reporting period was not. After an earnings warning in July 2000, the share price fell 42%, showing the capital-market pressure the company faced; that does not establish the pressure as sufficient justification for illegal conduct.

After media scrutiny and regulatory investigations emerged, the initial internal investigation did not uncover the problem. In 2003 the board asked an independent law firm to investigate further, which found 23 boxes of previously missed documents and more electronic evidence. The investigation involved finance executives and ultimately implicated Kumar, Richards and others. The case describes executives misleading the investigation, and the head of internal legal later admitted to directing executives to mislead it. "We already asked the company's lawyers" cannot be treated as sufficient for an effective independent review.

Richards pleaded guilty in 2006 and was sentenced to seven years in prison. In 2008 he wrote from prison to Eugene Soltes, then a student. He explained how analyst expectations fed into internal targets, and how targets shaped teams, organizational structure and incentives; he also discussed how self-identity and recognition for performance can be stronger drivers than money, and stressed that managers often operate in grey areas. The letter preserves his explanations and self-understanding; it is not an independent conclusion about his conduct.

The professor asks us to assess how serious the conduct was, then to judge whether that changes if the same numbers were achieved through flexibility GAAP allows, and finally to put ourselves in his shoes and propose a different course of action and its consequences. The focus of the class is not only recognising the wrongdoing, but whether you can adopt a workable arrangement at the moment of greatest pressure.

Core Tensions

  1. The choice between admitting a missed quarterly target and changing the reporting period to preserve an image of success. The short-term cost is real and cannot be eliminated by rewriting the facts.
  2. A real transaction and truthful reporting are requirements at two different levels.
  3. Legitimate room for judgement and intent to mislead must be distinguished item by item; "everyone does it" cannot substitute for analysis.
  4. Understanding the pressure and holding people accountable can both hold true; explaining the mechanism does not absolve individual choice.
  5. There is a gap between control responsibilities on paper and whether they are carried out in the face of real incentives.
