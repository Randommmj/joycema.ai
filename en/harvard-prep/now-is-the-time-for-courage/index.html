Now Is the Time for Courage

ClassFri, Oct 16 11:00–12:30 (Boston time) · Innovation · Ranjay Gulati

Read the prep essay →

The Professor's Questions

  1. What does courage mean to you?
  2. Can we learn to be courageous and if so, how?
  3. How can organizations become more courageous and what role can leaders play in doing so?

My Answers

Q1. What does courage mean to you?

My one-line answer: courage is paying a real price, while I am still afraid, for something I have decided is worth it. Three parts matter to me — the price, the fear, and the decision.

  1. The price has to be real. Gulati's definition is "a willingness to take bold, risky action to serve a purpose you perceive to be worthy, in the face of an abiding fear." I would underline risky. CVS gave up $2 billion of sales to stop selling tobacco. Haugen gave up a lucrative job. If nothing is at stake, it is not courage — it is just an opinion. This is also why I distrust "bold" statements that cost the speaker nothing.
  2. The fear stays. Mandela: courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. The article is careful here — the goal is not to stop being afraid but to act with the fear still in the room. Foulkes said walking away from a business that size was "a very scary thing," and they did it anyway. So for me courage is not a feeling. It is a behaviour you can observe: did the person act, or not?
  3. It is a decision, not a trait. Knight's distinction matters: when the odds can be calculated, what you need is good analysis, not courage. Courage starts where the spreadsheet stops — in uncertainty, where the outcomes themselves are unknown. At that point the brain's default is freeze or flight, and courage is the choice to take back agency and move. The article calls this reclaiming agency; I would call it taking responsibility for a decision nobody can prove right in advance.

Two things courage is not, for me. It is not recklessness — Aristotle put courage between cowardice and rashness, and Gulati agrees that "leaders should not take outsize risks." And it is not loud. Most of the courage I have seen in business is quiet: a founder going to survival salary for the fourth time (Bajpai), a plant manager sending a team to inspect a damaged reactor with a whiteboard as the only tool (Masuda).

In my own writing, long before this course, the three words I kept coming back to were being brave, caring, and being serious. Reading this article I would now reorder them: caring comes first — it is what makes something worth a price — and courage is what you do about it.

Q2. Can we learn to be courageous, and if so, how?

Yes — and the article's strongest evidence is not the research, it is the structure of the playbook: five strategies, each broken into three concrete, repeatable moves. Fifteen things you can practise. Nothing on the list requires a special personality.

Why I believe it is learnable:

  • The science says so. From Socrates to Mencius the question was open; modern research across psychology, sociology, neuroscience and healthcare lands on the side of "learnable." People who never thought of themselves as brave became so when the situation called for it.
  • The stories say so. Sully's training never covered a double engine failure, yet "my entire life had been preparation to handle that particular moment." The Taj staff were not hired for heroism; they went through 18 months of onboarding and then "fell back on that training." Courage looked like character from outside, but it was built by repetition.
  • The 9% says so. In the 2008 study, the companies that invested while cutting were not a different species. They had different habits.

How — my own ordering of the fifteen moves, from easiest to start with to hardest:

  1. Start with the body (Stay calm). Sleep, food, water; a ritual before the hard moment (Jobs' mirror question, Bernanke facing the clock); and the reframe: write down the pessimistic, realistic and optimistic version of what you face. Fink's line — "we have lived through moments like this before" — is a reframe you can borrow on day one.
  2. Hunt the risks (Positive narrative). Make a list of what you are actually afraid of. The point is not to get risk to zero; it is to turn a fog into a list. Fink built a whole company (Aladdin) on this; a founder can do it with one page.
  3. Name the purpose. Ask what this decision is for. CVS's $2 billion became bearable because 25,000 pharmacists could be proud of it. A moral quest is not decoration — it is what makes the price payable.
  4. Train deliberately in your own craft (Cultivate confidence). Competence is the raw material of confidence. Bandura's self-efficacy is not "I am brave," it is "I know what I can do with the skills I have." Pick the core skill of your role and drill it until it is second nature; then widen the tool kit (delegation, transparent debate, mental endurance).
  5. Focus on what you control. Break the big fear into small, ownable problems — "tariffs" into "change suppliers faster, tweak pricing." Saban: "We're not going to talk about what we're going to accomplish. We're going to talk about how we're going to do it."
  6. Take small steps, through two-way doors (Take small steps). Evaluate objectively, let meaning emerge, interpret in motion. Masuda's team never made one big decision at Daini; they made a sequence of small, reversible ones and corrected twice. This is the move that most changes what "bold" means: bold is not big; bold is early and reversible.
  7. Build the team around the fear (Find connection). One ally who believes in you (Nair's aunts, Nooyi), one source of tangible resources (Ixigo's bridge money), and at least one honest critic ("listen to the complainer"). Courage is a team sport; design the team before you need it.

The hardest of the fifteen, in my view, is "accept feedback" — because it means letting someone else judge the decision you are already afraid of. The easiest is self-care, and it is the one most leaders skip.

Q3. How can organizations become more courageous, and what role can leaders play?

An organization becomes courageous the same way a person does — through the same five strategies — except that in an organization every one of them can be designed into a system, and the leader has one job nobody else can do: go first, and pay visibly.

Mapping the five strategies onto organizational design:

Strategy What it looks like as a system Example in the article
Positive narrative A shared risk map everyone can see (not a risk register that only legal reads); a stated purpose that the hard decisions are explicitly tied to Aladdin; Fink's annual letter; CVS tying $2B to 25,000 pharmacists
Cultivate confidence Long, serious onboarding and drilling of the core craft; a wide tool kit taught, not assumed; "focus on process" as a management language Taj's 18-month onboarding; Nick Saban; Schultz and Knudstorp going back to basics
Take small steps A decision taxonomy: which decisions are two-way doors (fast, delegated, reversible) and which are one-way (slow, senior, deliberate); whiteboards before plans Amazon's two-way doors; Masuda's whiteboard
Find connection Safety nets (bridge capital, reserves, legal/medical support) so people can afford to act; institutionalized dissent — transparent debate, gemba walks, "listen to the complainer" Ixigo's investors; Luminate; Foulkes' debate culture; Culp; Lego
Stay calm Rest treated as operational necessity, not weakness; rituals before high-stakes moments; a habit of writing the pessimistic / realistic / optimistic case Nair's oxygen mask; Bernanke's clock; Fink's reframe

What only the leader can do:

  1. Define courage as a value out loud, then behave as if it costs something. Gulati's own conclusion is that bravery "must be learned and taught to others." In the 2023 article he said the cultural barrier — loss aversion — is overcome by "explicitly cultivating courage as a cultural value, making courageous behaviour the norm, with leaders modelling it." Pennington's "not investing is riskier" and Minicucci's "we want to be aggressive" are the leader saying the sentence first, in the quarter when it was hardest to say.
  2. Pay first. Foulkes' team debated for a long time and did not all agree. The decision became a source of pride only after it cost $2 billion. A leader who asks for courage while taking none is asking for compliance.
  3. Lower the cost of being brave for everyone else. Most people in an organization are not afraid of the market; they are afraid of their boss. Two-way doors, honest post-mortems without blame, and a visible safety net make bold action cheap enough that ordinary people will take it. The Taj employees were brave because the organization had made "guests first" the default, not an exception.
  4. Hunt risks in public. Put the fears on the whiteboard. Masuda did not tell his team "stay calm"; he gave them data to write down. An organization that names its risks is less frightened than one that whispers about them.
  5. Build dissent into the calendar. Gemba walks, complainer sessions, red-team reviews of the leader's own narrative. The point of accepting feedback is not humility — it is that validated interpretations produce more confident action.
  6. Protect the body of the organization. Sleep, pace, recovery. A team running on fumes will default to freeze. The leader sets the pace, and most set it too fast to be brave.

The cost: an organization that practises courage will make more visible mistakes. Two-way doors mean walking back through some of them in public. The leader has to absorb that — which is itself the courage the organization is learning from.

And Cynthia Carroll (10-10) is the whole playbook in one person — a moral quest (zero harm), a visible price (shutting the mines), allies and critics, and small steps after the big one.

Case Summary

The article opens with a discovery the author never saw coming. During the 2008 financial crisis, Gulati, Nitin Nohria and Franz Wohlgezogen studied how 4,700 public companies came through the three previous recessions (this is Roaring Out of Recession, which we read for the October 14 class). They found that roughly 9% of companies emerged from each recession stronger than they went in — not just because they cut costs cleverly, but because at the same time they took calculated risks to invest in growth. As the author puts it, they had "inadvertently documented the enormous difference that courage makes in business." For the next decade and more he turned to a different question: facing the same hard circumstances, why do some leaders dare to move while others don't?

He starts by defining courage. Courage is "the willingness to take bold, risky action in the face of persistent fear, for a purpose one considers worthy." It might mean taking on a physical challenge, making yourself emotionally vulnerable, staking your social or financial standing on a moral conviction, or publicly championing a new, unorthodox idea. He quotes Mandela: courage is not the absence of fear but the triumph over it. From Socrates, Plato and Aristotle to Mencius, philosophers have long argued over whether bravery is an innate virtue or something that can be taught; modern research in psychology, sociology, economics, neuroscience and medicine backs the latter — plenty of people who never thought of themselves as brave have acted bravely when the situation demanded it. Yet almost no one has systematically studied "how a person can become braver." So he pulled together the existing literature, archival research and more than 200 in-depth interviews to distill a playbook for how "anyone can be more fearless at work."

Then he draws a line: courage is for the part that risk management can't solve. Many experts say the way to overcome the anxiety of uncertainty is risk management — Kathleen Reardon calls courageous action a "smart gamble," built on "careful weighing and preparation." Running a cost-benefit analysis does lower the odds of a bad outcome. But the economist Frank Knight long ago distinguished "risk," whose probabilities can be estimated, from "uncertainty," which can neither be fully enumerated nor estimated. Under VUCA conditions, the possible outcomes themselves are unknown; most people feel a loss of control, which triggers fear, and they then either freeze or take flight. What sets courageous leaders apart is that they can seize back the initiative and choose the third response — fight. At the time of writing, 76% of S&P 500 companies had mentioned "uncertainty" on their earnings calls, up from 37% three months earlier.

Next come five strategies, each with three concrete practices, illustrated with stories.

Strategy one: build a positive narrative. The first practice is "risk hunting": not driving risk down to zero, but identifying the unknowns one by one so the world looks more orderly. BlackRock's Larry Fink built his whole career on this — his first employee, Charlie Hallac, was tasked with developing a comprehensive risk-assessment platform, later called Aladdin, which became the gold standard of the financial industry. In his 2025 annual letter, he pointed out that US$25 trillion was sitting idle in US bank accounts and money-market funds, while global public and private infrastructure would need about US$68 trillion over the next 25 years — figures like these, and finer-grained analysis, gave management the confidence to make three big acquisitions in late 2024: Global Infrastructure Partners (US$170 billion in assets, more than 100 countries), private-markets data firm Preqin, and private-credit manager HPS. The second practice is "find a moral mission": reframe the work as the pursuit of some value. Facebook whistleblower Frances Haugen said she "did what I believed was necessary to save the lives of people" endangered by Facebook putting profits first. In 2014, CVS's Helena Foulkes and her team decided to stop selling tobacco, giving up US$2 billion in sales and risking a backlash from consumers and investors — "We debated it for a long time, and not everyone agreed... walking away from such a big piece of business was scary," but "we had 25,000 pharmacists at the time, and for them and for all of us, working at a company that made such a bold decision really created a sense of pride." The third practice is "draw on faith": research shows entrepreneurs spend more time praying than the general population and are more likely to believe in a God who responds to them; people who believe they'll be lucky feel a stronger sense of agency. Cadbury, Rockefeller, Sam Walton and Aflac's Dan Amos have all spoken publicly about the role of faith; former PepsiCo CEO Indra Nooyi kept a statue of the elephant-headed god Ganesha in her office — "when the stress was unbearable... I'd close my eyes and think of a temple... and suddenly feel I could take on the whole world."

Strategy two: cultivate confidence. Confidence comes from what Bandura called self-efficacy — "your belief in what you can do with the skills you have, under a variety of circumstances." The first practice is "deliberate training": practice best practices until they become second nature. Sully Sullenberger's pilot training had no lesson on "how to ditch in the Hudson after a dual engine failure," but he said, "I'd been preparing for that moment my whole life." Bernanke's academic career studying the Great Depression equipped him to handle 2008; GE's Larry Culp carried out GE's complex restructuring on the lean expertise he built over 25 years at Danaher, 13 of them as CEO. Staff at Mumbai's Taj Mahal Palace hotel stayed at their posts through the days-long siege of the 2008 terrorist attacks; every one of them had gone through 18 months of onboarding, learning not just the job but a "guest first" culture — during the attack they saw themselves as guardians of the company and its guests. The second practice is "build a big toolkit": meta-tools of all kinds for thinking, solving problems and staying resilient. NASA's Terry Virts said boot camps on mental endurance were as useful as technical and physical training; Foulkes's personal tool was delegation and her team tool was transparent debate — "deep conversations about paradox and complexity... letting people say what excites them and what makes them nervous... and turning those competing emotions into a new, creative solution." The third practice is "focus on what you can control": break a big question like "how do we respond to changing tariff policy" into small ones like "switch suppliers faster, fine-tune pricing"; or simply get the operational basics right. In sport it's called "focus on process, not outcome" — Nick Saban told his players, "We don't talk about what we're going to achieve; we talk about how we're going to do it." Starbucks' Howard Schultz got through 2008 by going back to the core coffeehouse experience; LEGO's Jørgen Vig Knudstorp engineered a turnaround by returning to the mission of bricks and creativity; Ixigo's Aloke Bajpai ran the company out of money at least four times — "Most companies would have shut down, but each time the founders put money back in and took subsistence salaries, investors gave bridge funding, many employees took big pay cuts, and most importantly, we kept shipping one of the most innovative products in the world."

Strategy three: take small steps. Many people equate boldness with big moves, but the leaders and organizations the author studied probe with small steps before they leap — what Karl Weick calls sensemaking. You let go of the need for a perfect plan and trust that the path will appear as you walk it. Step one is "assess objectively": the trigger is often a disruptive event that creates uncertainty, and the instinct is to freeze or flee, but you need to practice mindfulness, observing both the external situation and your own emotional reaction, and ask: What am I facing? Whom can I call on for help? What can I do right now to understand the situation? When the 2011 tsunami hit Fukushima, Naohiro Masuda, head of the Daini (No. 2) plant, gathered his team around a whiteboard, wrote down the data they knew, and analyzed what might go wrong and how to stop it — a 55-foot wave had struck a plant designed for only 17 feet. Step two is "let meaning emerge": gather every clue you can, however trivial it seems, piece them into an explanation, form hypotheses, pick the most plausible one, then keep gathering and revising. As Weick says, "Sensemaking is not about truth and getting it right. Instead, it is about continued redrafting of an emerging story so that it becomes more comprehensive, incorporates more of the observed data, and is more resilient in the face of criticism." When the main control panel went dark, Masuda sent people out at personal risk to inspect the damaged equipment on site, and from that hypothesized that the cooling systems had lost power and that they needed to bring materials to repair them and find an alternative energy source. Step three is "interpret through action": act on the hypothesis, expose yourself to new clues, and carry on or change course. The key is to take steps that keep options open — what Amazon calls "two-way doors": you can walk through, and you can walk back out. Masuda's team first tried to draw power from the only building on site that still had electricity; that proved technically too hard, so they switched to the one generator still working, and redirected their efforts after judging that another reactor was in greater danger. Small steps, observation, learning, iteration — in the end they averted disaster, which Fukushima Daiichi (No. 1) did not.

Strategy four: find connection. Contrary to the popular story, lone heroes are rare. Courage is a team sport; behind every brave leader there is usually a group of less visible but vitally important supporters, plus a set of constructive critics. The first practice is "seek emotional support from allies": when the people around you believe you're capable of courage, you start to believe it too; relationships also affirm your identity and your obligations to others, making you feel more responsible for acting. Chanel CEO Leena Nair says that when people doubted her abilities as a woman, or as an HR leader from a "soft skills" background, she would think of the older women in her family whose own ambitions had been stifled but who had complete faith in her — "they'd say, give Leena that chance, she's smart, she's talented." Her mentor Nooyi told her not to think about her limitations but about the key strengths she could bring to the role. The second practice is "secure resources": money and people are a safety net. Bajpai's bridge funding; Luminate (the organization of eBay founder Omidyar) and Whistleblower Aid behind Haugen. Nooyi: "You can't know everything. Bring in the experts and let them teach you." The third practice is "accept feedback": you can't surround yourself only with cheerleaders; you need critics who assess you honestly. One of Knudstorp's mottos at LEGO was "listen to the people who complain"; Culp is known for "gemba walks" — managers walking the shop floor to hear directly from frontline workers.

Strategy five: stay calm. What psychologists call "emotion regulation" is the ability to influence how you feel when the "freeze, flight, fight" response is triggered. The first practice is "self-care": sleep, nutrition, hydration — "you can't think clearly when your tank is empty." Nair says, "Put on your own oxygen mask first, so you have the energy to play all your roles." The second practice is "embrace rituals": Japanese samurai and Norse Vikings had pre-battle rituals; every morning Steve Jobs made his bed, showered, then looked in the mirror and asked, "If today were the last day of my life, would I be happy with what I'm about to do today?"; some entrepreneurs wear a lucky sweater or recite a mantra before every fundraising or customer pitch; Bernanke, following Greenspan's habit, always sat facing the clock at Fed meetings; Schultz relies on regular meditation. The third practice is "reframe the situation": redefine what the uncertainty means in the long run for you and your organization, thinking through pessimistic, realistic and optimistic versions in turn — fully acknowledging the danger while dialing down your reaction to it. Fink wrote in his annual letter: "We've been through moments like this before. Somehow, over the long run, we always figure things out." Foulkes: "My mind sometimes goes to 'what could go wrong' — that's probably the benefit of business school training — but it's just as important to ask, 'What good could happen?'" Bajpai reframed the Covid pandemic as yet another crisis that forced him to conserve cash, while it also pushed the company to explore new growth, including acquiring train and bus ticketing businesses — which laid the groundwork for Ixigo's successful IPO in 2024.

It closes with a judgment. As change and disruption accelerate, courage — or its absence — will make or break careers, organizations and societies. That doesn't mean leaders should take excessive risks, "but most of us tend to be too timid rather than too reckless." After hundreds of interviews, the author is convinced that bravery is a skill every businessperson today must learn and teach to others; solving big problems like climate change, economic inequality and political polarisation will require summoning a kind of collective courage. "A little more boldness can go a long way."

Core Tensions

  1. Fear is the natural response, yet courage demands a third response that goes against instinct. Uncertainty → loss of control → fear is the nervous system's default setting; freeze and flight are both instinctive, fight is a choice. The whole article is an answer to "how do you make that choice possible." This is the common root of all three of the professor's questions.
  2. What risk management can handle, and what it can't. Cost-benefit analysis works for "risk" (probabilities can be estimated) but not for "uncertainty" (outcomes are unknown). People who lean too heavily on analysis freeze under VUCA — because the analysis can't give them an answer. Courage doesn't replace analysis; it takes the baton once analysis has run its course.
  3. Bold = big moves, or bold = small steps? Intuition says bravery is a single great leap; the research says brave leaders probe with small steps first, keep the doors two-way, and correct as they go. Masuda's team never made one "big decision," only a chain of small decisions that kept getting corrected.
  4. Lone hero vs. team sport. In cultural narratives, courage is a personal trait; the article says every brave person has allies, resources and critics behind them. That means organizational courage can be designed — rather than relying solely on hiring brave people.
  5. Too timid vs. too reckless. Aristotle placed courage at the mean between cowardice and recklessness. Gulati's judgment is that in reality most people's problem is the former. That determines which way an organization should push — what most companies need is not more risk control but more permission.
  6. Values become a source of courage only when they cost something. CVS's moral mission "created a sense of pride" precisely because it cost US$2 billion. Free values don't produce courage (the same logic as Cynthia Carroll shutting down the mine in the 10-10 session: only decisions that come at a cost are believed).
