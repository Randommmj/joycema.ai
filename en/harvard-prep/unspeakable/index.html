The Professor's Questions

  1. Read the HBS note "Note on Difficult Conversations in the Family Enterprise," and the introductory chapter of Unspeakable (PDF attached).
  2. Before our session, think of one conversation you've been avoiding with a colleague, a family member, a business partner, or a successor. Write down who it's with, why you haven't had it yet, and what waiting is costing you

Case Summary

In the introduction to Unspeakable, Luis Garcia founded a successful dry-cleaning business, yet for years was unwilling to talk about money, succession or death. The arrangements he made twenty years earlier had their reasons at the time: his elder daughter lived locally and was well placed to take over the business; his son was just starting a career in education and needed help with housing; his younger daughter's finances were unstable and called for the restrictions of a trust. Over the next twenty years, the elder daughter and the son moved out of state, while the younger daughter stayed in the business and looked after her father — but the arrangements were never updated, nor discussed with the children. The documents revealed after the funeral took all three children by surprise. According to the author, within a year they were no longer speaking to one another.

The introduction also describes Alex and Maya. Alex, ashamed of being unable to find a job, was unwilling to ask for help; Maya's manager, out of consideration for her having just returned from parental leave, let her miss a promotion opportunity without asking her. The author uses these stories to show that silence can come from shame, kindness or concern about boundaries, yet still shapes decisions and relationships.

The HBS note offers three sample conversations. Steve wants to take over, but his mother assumed he didn't want to join the business; John, the other candidate his mother had in mind, had never been asked what he wanted either. After the conversation, they agree to hold a third-generation meeting in two weeks. Jason doesn't know how much longer his father can keep supporting him; through the conversation he learns about his own spending, and the two agree on budgeting coaching and a follow-up in a month. Crystal took a suddenly proposed prenuptial agreement as a sign that her father didn't trust her partner, and only later learned it was a family-wide arrangement; they agree that her father and the lawyer will take part in explaining it.

The materials don't portray these conversations as solving everything in one go. The HBS note emphasises follow-up meetings, a neutral third party when needed, and reflection and rescheduling after a conversation goes off the rails. The appendix covers nine categories of questions: family, governance, the operating company, education, the family office, estate planning and inheritance, philanthropy, legacy, and open communication.

Core Tensions

  1. There is a gap between being willing to raise an issue and being able to move arrangements forward.
  2. The trade-off between keeping short-term peace and bearing long-term uncertainty. Not talking doesn't make problems go away on its own.
  3. The line between being considerate of others and deciding for them. Well-meaning inferences still need to give the person a chance to correct them.
  4. The difference between seeking clarity and demanding that others accept your solution. A conversation can first clarify the facts and next steps, without needing to align every position on the spot.
  5. Having the courage to face known problems and identifying unknown ones are two different abilities. Not every unresolved issue should be read as avoidance.
