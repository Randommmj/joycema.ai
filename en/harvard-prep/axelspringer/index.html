The Professor's Questions

The Axel Springer (A–C) case series explores the radical transformation of a legacy German media company into a global digital force. Under the leadership of CEO Mathias Döpfner, Axel Springer evolved from a conservative postwar tabloid empire into a billion-dollar player in digital journalism and online classifieds. The case spans nearly two decades of strategic reinvention through M&A, cultural change, and governance restructuring.

  1. How has Axel Springer changed under Mathias Döpfner's leadership?
  2. What are the strategic, financial, and cultural implications of acquiring Business Insider vs. Huffington Post?
  3. What should Döpfner do in 2015? Why?

Case Summary

July 23, 2015, Tegel Airport, Berlin. Mathias Döpfner was sitting at the gate waiting for a phone call. He was about to fly to London to sign the agreement to buy the Financial Times (FT) from Pearson, a deal worth more than US$1 billion — the core step in Axel Springer's strategy of "internationalizing the media business and entering the English-speaking world." Everyone wanted to board; he said no, wait for final confirmation from Pearson's board. The call came: during the board meeting another offer had arrived, and they had no choice but to accept it. The FT went to Nikkei. A contract negotiated for a year had been taken by the other side and bid up 10% — "take it or leave it." Deputy CEO Jan Bayer recalled that the two of them talked about it all summer, then met in Berlin and said: the international direction is right; we're going to America.

Rewind seventy years. Axel Springer was founded in Hamburg in 1946 by the journalist-entrepreneur of the same name. Hamburger Abendblatt in 1948, Bild in 1952, the acquisition of Die Welt in 1953, the acquisition of the Ullstein publishing house (including the Berliner Morgenpost) in 1959. By the 1960s it was Germany's most important news publisher. The company put its headquarters beside the Berlin Wall at Checkpoint Charlie, and in 1967 it set down the "Essentials" — five editorial principles still in use today: freedom and democracy, Israel's right to exist, the transatlantic alliance between the US and Europe, the free market, and opposition to extremism. In the 1960s this made it a target of left-wing protest; the street leading to its headquarters was later named after the protest leader Rudi Dutschke.

Bild is the most-read newspaper in Europe, and was still running topless pin-up photos as late as 2012; Schröder said "all you need to govern Germany is Bild, Bild am Sonntag and television." Welt has a far smaller circulation and is the group's intellectual flagship. In 1985 Axel Springer listed 49% of the shares and died the same year, leaving control to his widow, Friede Springer. From then on, day-to-day management was handed to professional CEOs and an executive board (Vorstand) of 3–5 people, with Friede sitting behind them as a supervisory board member and major shareholder.

In 2002, Döpfner took over. He was 39, had studied musicology, had spent 16 years in journalism, the last four as editor-in-chief of Welt. Friede acknowledged the doubts at the time: "They said he was too young, a music critic with no business experience... I said, I want him. And I have the majority of votes." His plan was simple: strengthen the core business, digitize, internationalize. He said that when he saw his first news website he understood "this will replace newspapers." The company was losing €200 million a year at the time, governance was unstable, and the culture needed to change — "there was nothing to lose and a lot to win." He cut 40% of the jobs, sold or shut down unprofitable assets, and within a year went from a €200 million loss to a profit of about €200 million. Almost everyone was against it: "This young man is destroying our business."

2004–2005: the first wall. They tried to buy ProSiebenSat.1, Germany's largest private TV group — bigger than Axel Springer was at the time. The team designed a multi-step deal of "tender offer for preferred shares + capital increase + merger"; the structure worked, but the German antitrust authority blocked it. Bayer called it a turning point: Döpfner concluded that they had to drastically accelerate digitization and internationalization, and use the print business to fund it while it was still making money.

Over the next decade, three tracks moved in parallel. First, digitizing news: Bild only launched its paywall, BildPlus (Bundesliga highlights, sweepstakes and slot-machine games plus news), in 2013; the same year, they sold a group of regional newspapers and magazines — including the paper the founder himself started in 1948 — to Funke for €920 million. Those assets had 2012 revenue of €512 million and EBITDA of €95 million, roughly 10x EBITDA; Bayer said it was "probably the last chance to exit," and Friede "understood immediately and agreed decisively — she always looks forward, never back." Second, classifieds: Döpfner saw early on that newspapers' real estate, jobs and car ads were being replaced by the Craigslists and Zillows, so he went shopping worldwide — StepStone, Immonet, aufeminin, Seloger, Totaljobs, Immoweb — merging them into horizontal groups for jobs, real estate and autos; analysts called Axel Springer "one of the most aggressive buyers of classifieds in Europe." Third, by 2014 more than half the group's revenue came from digital businesses.

The way it bought companies became a philosophy in itself. Döpfner didn't do deep integration; he called it the "United Artists model" — after the film company Chaplin and others founded in 1919 to protect their creative independence: "I respect entrepreneurs, and I want Axel Springer to be their home. As long as they deliver the plan and the results, let them go their own way." Bayer's metaphor was a jazz band: improvising within a clear framework, with no preset direction. "We don't believe in top-down synergies. You can treat Axel Springer as a buffet — knowledge, technology, product, legal, M&A — but you don't have to." The deal structure became standardized as a result: founders keep a substantial stake and later exit via a Put/Call — 12x EBITDA if they beat the plan, 8x if they miss. Deputy head of M&A Andreas Noth said: "When founders want to sell 100% at once and walk away, we're usually suspicious — they may not believe in the company's potential."

The industry was running in the same direction at the same time. Nikkei bought the FT; News Corp bought Myspace, Move and REA; Schibsted bought Blocket and Leboncoin; Bertelsmann integrated Gruner + Jahr; in the US, Gannett, the New York Times and Tribune bet on digital subscriptions; PE firms like Alden bought distressed newspapers to restructure them. The US digital media market was hot in 2015: NBCUniversal invested a combined US$400 million in Buzzfeed and Vox; Buzzfeed was valued at US$1.5 billion, about 13x revenue; Hearst and Disney bought 10% of Vice at a US$2.5 billion valuation, about 5x revenue; none was profitable. Forbes sold to a Hong Kong investor group in 2014 for US$475 million, about 3x revenue and 22x EBITDA.

Two candidates. Business Insider: founded in 2007 by former DoubleClick CEO Kevin Ryan and Henry Blodget (Blodget had a history from his Wall Street analyst days of being investigated by the SEC, barred from the industry and fined US$4 million), purely digital business and tech news, known for high output, SEO and social distribution, and also criticized for "clickbait headlines, more ads than content, and slideshows to farm clicks"; about 75% of revenue from advertising, plus a small B2B subscription research product, BI Intelligence; venture-backed, with shareholders including Bezos and Andreessen; in 2013 AOL offered about US$100–150 million and the board rejected it, asking around US$250 million at the time. In January 2015, Axel Springer led its Series G, US$20 million for 9% and a board seat — Noth called it "a foot in the door": invest a small amount first, get to know the management and founders, understand the culture. By mid-2015 BI had nearly 90 million monthly unique visitors and revenue approaching US$50 million.

Huffington Post: founded in 2005 by Arianna Huffington, Kenneth Lerer, Jonah Peretti and Andrew Breitbart, a liberal-leaning news and commentary site that became a large general-interest site through aggregation, original content and a high-output model built on thousands of unpaid bloggers; by 2015 it had 13 international editions (UK, Canada, France, Germany, Spain, Brazil, India, Japan, Korea, Australia) and had just launched an Arabic edition. AOL bought it in 2011 for US$315 million, when annual revenue was about US$30 million. Its business model relied entirely on advertising, with no paid product; in 2012 it invested in the video platform HuffPost Live. 2014 revenue was estimated at US$146 million, still loss-making (international expansion, newsroom, video); 2015 revenue was expected to be US$168 million, with EBITDA over US$10 million. In 2015 Verizon bought AOL for US$4.4 billion and was rumored to be spinning off HuffPost for about US$1 billion — roughly 6–7x revenue.

The case stops here: Döpfner and his team, recovering from the FT setback, have to decide — buy Business Insider or HuffPost? And this time, how do they make sure the deal doesn't fly away at the last minute?

Core Tensions

  1. Scale vs. fit (the professor's decision question): HuffPost has US$168 million in revenue, 13 international editions and has just turned profitable — a one-step entry into the top tier of global English-language media — but it costs US$1 billion, about 100x EBITDA, with 15% growth, pure advertising and no founders; BI has about US$50 million in revenue, growth doubling, the seed of a B2B subscription business, founders in place, Axel Springer already on the board, and an overall valuation of a little over US$400 million, but it is small, its reputation is controversial, and it isn't profitable.
  2. The "go to America" strategy vs. the conservative Essentials: Axel Springer's identity is conservative, pro-Israel and free-market; HuffPost's identity is liberal. United Artists allows editorial independence, but a mismatch of brand and political stance is the kind of thing that makes headlines every day. On the other hand, the third Essential, the "transatlantic alliance," is itself a reason to go to America.
  3. United Artists autonomy vs. actually building a global platform: "Not believing in top-down synergies" protects entrepreneurs, but how does a website with US$50 million in revenue become a global player? The autonomy model requires the acquired company to have its own growth engine and founders — and that requirement is itself a filter on targets.
  4. The lifespan of the print cash cow vs. the speed of digitization: Bild only got a paywall in 2013; the regional papers were only sold in 2013, and that was "probably the last window." There isn't much time left to fund the transition with print, so after losing the FT, "wait a bit" isn't an option.
  5. Execution discipline after the FT: After a year of negotiation, they were snatched away by +10%. The next deal has to be fast and certain, but a "wounded" buyer is the most likely to overpay just to close. How can speed and price discipline hold at the same time?
  6. News vs. classifieds: The money is in classifieds (US$1.44 billion in disclosed acquisitions); news is the identity and the mission. Spending US$1 billion on a news brand — is that paying for identity or for returns?
