The Professor's Questions

  1. What are the key resources and capabilities behind Golden Goose’s success? Are they scalable?
  2. What challenges or constraints does Golden Goose face in growing its business, especially given its luxury positioning?
  3. Should Golden Goose deepen its focus on sneakers and Co-Creation, or pursue new directions for growth? Think about the potential and trade-offs of each of the three strategic paths: a. Sneakers & Co-Creation b. Ready-to-wear and accessories c. Geographic expansion
  4. Which of these options best balances growth potential with brand coherence and executional feasibility? What would need to be true for your chosen strategy to succeed?

My Answers

Q1. What are the key resources and capabilities behind Golden Goose’s success? Are they scalable?

Golden Goose's advantage is a connected system: recognizable shoes, a personal experience, direct customer access and the ability to learn from customers. Parts of it scale more easily than others.

The distressed look and a small set of recognizable styles give the product a clear identity. Italian craftsmanship and a disciplined price position make that identity credible. Co-Creation lets customers participate, so the value is partly in the experience and attachment, not only the physical shoe. Stores and CRM connect the company directly to customers. Staff collect information that helps with recommendations, inventory and store location decisions. These activities reinforce one another. (pp.3–8)

The results support the system: FY2024 revenue was €654.6m, with a 74.4% gross margin. There were 215 stores at year end. However, a margin alone does not prove that competitors cannot copy the model. Nor does a customer buying 1.8 pairs a year establish the retention rate of the full customer base. (Exhibits 2, 12; p.4)

Core designs, data systems and inventory rules can scale through repeatable processes. Friendly staff, skilled artisans and meaningful customer interaction need training, time and local judgment. The academy helps, but it cannot instantly create experienced artisans. I would scale the operating standards and training while leaving room for individual expression. The scarce resource is consistent human attention, not simply more store space.

Q2. What challenges or constraints does Golden Goose face in growing its business, especially given its luxury positioning?

Growth must preserve the reason customers pay a premium, while funding a more complex operation. I see four constraints.

First, wider distribution can weaken distinctiveness if customers see the same product and experience everywhere. Exclusivity need not mean hostility or very few stores, but the company must preserve craft, recognizable design and personal service. Second, Co-Creation consumes space and skilled labor. More demand can produce queues and uneven quality if artisan capacity does not grow with it. Third, category expansion adds sizes, design decisions, inventory and markdown risk. Sneakers still represented about 87% of 2024 sales, so customer permission to become a broad lifestyle brand remains a question. (pp.4–9; Exhibit 7)

Fourth, growth is already placing demands on profit and working capital. FY2024 revenue rose 11.5%, while EBIT fell slightly from €132.5m to €131.7m. Inventory rose 29.5%, to €147m. These figures warrant investigation; they do not by themselves prove weak stores or obsolete stock. (Exhibits 1–2)

I would therefore watch full-price sell-through, store contribution, service quality, inventory aging and cash payback together. A store that looks successful in revenue can still tie up cash or dilute the experience.

Q3. Should Golden Goose deepen its focus on sneakers and Co-Creation, or pursue new directions for growth? Think about the potential and trade-offs of each of the three strategic paths: a. Sneakers & Co-Creation b. Ready-to-wear and accessories c. Geographic expansion

I would put sneakers and Co-Creation first, use selective geographic expansion second, and keep apparel and accessories as measured experiments. This is a sequence, not a permanent rejection of the other routes.

Path Growth potential Trade-off and evidence needed
Sneakers and Co-Creation More value from a proven product and customer relationship; Co-Creation starts at $150 Larger space and more artisans must create enough incremental contribution; measure waiting time, extra margin and later repeat purchases
Ready-to-wear and accessories More purchases per customer and less dependence on footwear Awareness is not demand; check full-price purchases, returns, stock turns and whether the brand has a distinctive product advantage
Geographic expansion Serve customers who already buy online or while traveling New countries bring duties, operating complexity and opening delays; use CRM demand evidence, local economics and management capacity

For apparel, Exhibit 10 shows €22m in 2021, €33m in 2022 and €38m in 2023; €11m for 2024 is only Q1. I would not annualize that quarter mechanically. For geographic expansion, the company's targets are a payback below two years and a 30% EBITDA margin. Neither should be replaced by a revenue-growth target. (pp.8–9; Exhibits 10–11)

The opportunity cost of my choice is slower diversification. I accept it because expanding every dimension at once would make it harder to diagnose what works and harder to protect execution quality.

Q4. Which of these options best balances growth potential with brand coherence and executional feasibility? What would need to be true for your chosen strategy to succeed?

A staged expansion of Co-Creation in proven stores offers the best balance. It strengthens the existing customer promise and uses capabilities already present, though it still needs proof of profitable scale.

Four assumptions must hold. Customers must pay for the experience without simply moving spending from other products. Extra contribution must cover rent, staff, training and working capital. The company must train artisans without losing service quality. Finally, the deeper experience must improve repeat purchase or customer advocacy, not just the first transaction.

I would test comparable stores with and without the added service, account for differences in location and customer mix, and track contribution per customer, contribution per square meter, wait time, rework, satisfaction and repeat purchases over a full buying cycle. My proposed decision rule is to expand only where the extra cash supports the investment and the experience remains strong. The case does not supply a validated numerical hurdle for a store expansion, so I would agree that hurdle before the test rather than invent a proven return.

If demand is strong but labor is the constraint, invest in training before adding more locations. If customers enjoy the experience but incremental economics are weak, keep it in selected flagship stores. If the concept succeeds, use CRM to identify the next geography. My classroom vote is: deepen first, replicate selectively, broaden categories after evidence.

Case Summary

Golden Goose was founded in 2000, initially combining the artisanal craftsmanship of the Venice region of Italy with the casual style of California to sell high-end lifestyle apparel. After it launched the Super-Star sneaker in 2007, footwear became the centre of business growth. The distressed finish made the product feel less like a showpiece that needed careful protection and more like something that already belonged to the wearer. (pp.2–3)

The brand later went through several rounds of private equity investment. DGPA came in in 2013, Ergon in 2015 and Carlyle in 2017, and Permira acquired it in 2020. As capital came in, the organisation also gradually professionalised: functions such as HR, merchandising, marketing and legal were built, and responsibilities once concentrated in the hands of a few managers were split up. From 2017, the company accelerated the build-out of its own stores and brought e-commerce operations in-house; at the time it had only six directly operated stores. (p.3)

Silvio Campara joined as commercial head in 2013 and became CEO in 2018. Through a small number of core sneaker styles, constantly changing colours and materials, Made in Italy, a distressed aesthetic and a pricing policy of rarely discounting, the company built a recognisable product system. What management wanted to create was a sense of belonging for customers, not just the distance of traditional luxury. (pp.3–4)

In 2019, the brand launched Co-Creation in Milan. Customers design details together with in-store artisans, turning a purchase into participation in creation. Later, Forward Stores offered repair, re-creation, resale and recycling services, and in 2024 it opened HAUS, adding community, events and artisan training. At the same time, CRM information fed into store location, product design, customer contact and inventory allocation, and sales staff also took on the job of accurately collecting customer feedback. (pp.5–8)

The business extended from sneakers into ready-to-wear, accessories and kidswear, but footwear still accounts for the vast majority of revenue. At the end of 2024 there were 215 stores in total, and full-year revenue in the exhibits was €654.6m. Greater scale did not remove the growth choices: keep expanding stores to deliver the sneaker co-creation service; double down on ready-to-wear and accessories; or open stores in new countries and cities. All three paths could deliver growth, and each draws on different resources: space, design and inventory capability, and cross-border operating capability. (pp.8–9; Exhibits 2, 7, 12)

Core Tensions

  1. Deepening existing strengths vs building new sources of growth. Sneakers still contribute about 87% of revenue, so doubling down has a solid base but also keeps category concentration risk; ready-to-wear growth could diversify revenue, but adds complexity in design, sizing and inventory.
  2. Warm, personal co-creation vs replicable expansion. Bigger stores can add more services, but artisan training and service quality don't grow automatically with floor space.
  3. Customer belonging vs covering more markets. Global store expansion can serve existing demand, but may also standardise the experience and dilute brand distinctiveness.
  4. Revenue growth vs profit and cash realisation. FY2024 revenue grew about 11.5%, EBIT fell slightly, and inventory grew faster; the next round of growth should prove out incremental profit and cash.
