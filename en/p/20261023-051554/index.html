Leadership: Jean-Claude Biver

Giving an old craft new meaning

Unit 3, Professor Jeff Polzer, Leadership: Jean-Claude Biver (A): The Reemergence of the Swiss Watch Industry.

Once timekeeping became cheaper and more accurate, the mechanical watch had to answer anew why people would still need it. Biver's story is about an old craft regaining its meaning — and about a forceful leader running into his own limits.

Here goes — enjoy:

Case Summary

Quartz technology transformed watchmaking. It was more accurate, used fewer parts and lent itself to mass production. The Swiss mechanical watch industry took a heavy blow, and many companies and craftsmen lost their place. Biver, who had worked at Audemars Piguet and Omega, came to believe that the future of the mechanical watch could be built on craftsmanship, history and emotional value.

In 1982, he and watchmaking partner Jacques Piquet bought Blancpain, a brand that had been dormant for years. The team sought out craftsmen, turned an old farmhouse into a workshop, and held firm to mechanical watchmaking, low inventory and made-to-order production, making the brand's history part of both the product and the way they worked.

In its first year, the company sold 97 watches for revenue of US$75,000; five years later, it was selling about 3,000 watches a year, with revenue of US$9.4 million. Biver didn't just reach out to consumers; he also built relationships with industry leaders, collectors and craftsmen. Old skills began to be valued again as capabilities worth cherishing.

Alongside the business's success, the team also had to deal with his emotions. He could be generous, passionate and quick to recognize others' contributions, but he could also fly into rages and publicly berate employees. A personal marital crisis further affected his work. After selling Blancpain in 1992, he soon found that wealth was no substitute for the meaning that a team and a mission had given him, so he asked to return to the Swatch Group.

Hayek had him take on the revival of Omega at the same time. Biver repaired the brand's messaging and, through a long-term partnership with Cindy Crawford and the Bond films, reconnected its heritage with contemporary consumers. From 1995 to 1999, Omega's revenue grew from US$350 million to more than US$900 million, with the group's resources also supporting the turnaround.

A serious illness in 2000, changes in his family, and a shift in his role within the group brought him to another choice. During a leave of absence, Hublot emerged as a possible new opportunity, but the company was still losing money, and its owners were worried about his management style. His past achievements did not make the decision about the next chapter of his life for him.

My Thinking

After technology changes, value has to be explained anew

Biver found the reasons why mechanical watches were still worth buying. Craftsmanship, history, aesthetics and identity can add up to a kind of value distinct from cheap, precise timekeeping.

My own take is that this requires the product to deliver on it. Without craftsmen, manufacturing and a consistent experience, history becomes mere packaging. Nor can we conclude from this that every old technology can be reborn; whether customers care, and whether they'll pay, still has to be tested.

Consistency has to run through every business choice

Brand messaging only has real power when it can coordinate design, production, distribution and communication. At Blancpain, the workshop, the sense of limited supply and the mechanical promise all reinforced one another; at Omega, history, product lines and long-term partnerships achieved consistency in a different way.

This also explains why you can't simply copy the same playbook. The scarcity logic of a small brand and the scaled revival of a group brand draw on different resources. What can be learned is how decisions are organized around a single customer promise.

A personal vision needs complementary capabilities to carry it

Craftsmen, partners, employees and institutional resources all took part in the success. Biver's way of expressing ideas and rallying people is easy to remember, but Piquet's craftsmanship, Hayek's backing, and the group's manufacturing and distribution mattered just as much.

I'd be wary of writing an industry's revival as a story of a single hero. Swatch's quartz products and industry consolidation are part of this history too. Only by understanding these conditions can we see how an idea travels from persuading others to reliable delivery.

High standards and harmful behavior need to be judged separately

Business success doesn't prove that rage is a necessary condition. Employees in the case describe passion and generosity, but also humiliation, threats and fear. Both kinds of evidence deserve to be taken seriously.

My own judgment is that you can keep intense debate about ideas while clearly opposing harmful ways of treating people. An apology without a change in behavior can hardly give employees stable expectations. The more a leader has achieved, the more they need someone who can calmly voice disagreement.

The next venture has to be compatible with life as it is now

Selling the company didn't automatically bring fulfillment, and starting over won't automatically solve every problem. At the end of the case, Biver has to weigh the meaning of the work, his family, his recovery and the real authority he'd have.

I'd support him in exploring the new opportunity, but only after spelling out the commitment, the role and the boundaries. Hublot is very small and still losing money; he can't assume it will work again just because he once revived a brand. For someone who has already proven what he can do, allowing himself to choose on his own terms may be harder than continuing to prove himself.

Takeaways for Arcus42

When tools change, explain anew what people are worth. The judgment, explanation, trust and accountability clients need have to be visible in concrete service, not just in an abstract narrative.

Let the promise discipline the delivery. However we express ourselves externally, we need corresponding information and action internally to back it up. Every inconsistency raises the cost of understanding.

Pair storytelling with the ability to verify. People who can articulate a direction and people who can find holes and check facts should support each other. A good story needs someone to keep checking it.

Protect disagreement even after success. When a leader has often been right in the past, the team more easily stops questioning. Keeping dissent sayable protects future judgment.

Before Class

My pre-class judgment is that what's most worth learning from Biver is his ability to rediscover value, build community and coordinate action. The behaviors that harmed employees should be left clearly in the reflection.

For a craft to endure, it needs someone to reinterpret it, and also people willing to calmly keep doing it well.

This piece is a personal learning analysis based on the course case. The people's experiences, business figures and management descriptions are limited to the period covered by the case, and no assessment is made of the current situation of the people or companies involved. Judgments of behavior are my personal pre-class views.
