Leadership Case

Lessons from C&S's Self-Managed Teams

September 24, 2025 · 6 min read

Case Summary

C&S Wholesale Grocers is a 70-year-old grocery wholesaler. In 1988 it landed a big contract with A&P supermarkets, and sales jumped from US$450 million to US$650 million. But with it came congested warehouses, more accidents and severe staff attrition (annual turnover among warehouse workers was close to 90%), and both productivity and quality fell.

CEO Rick Cohen found that the traditional warehouse model of "division of labour + layer upon layer of supervision" was inefficient, blurred responsibility and was costly. He tried "self-managed teams": small teams responsible for an entire order (picking, loading, checking, signing off), choosing their own members, supervising themselves, and accountable for performance as a team. The experiment showed higher productivity, better quality, and workers who felt a greater sense of achievement. But Cohen faced a decision: roll it out across the board? How should it be implemented? Was now the right time?

Timeline: 1918: Israel Cohen founds C&S. 1958: wins the Big D supermarket chain account and begins to grow sharply. 1975: a union strike nearly bankrupts the company → it moves to Brattleboro, Vermont, and becomes non-union. 1981: a new warehouse opens; annual sales are US$98 million, the target is US$300 million, and it ultimately reaches US$400 million in 1986. 1987: Cohen becomes CEO. 1988: signs A&P; sales jump to US$650 million, but the warehouse is overloaded and accidents and turnover are high. Summer 1988: Cohen tests "self-managed teams," with positive results. Autumn 1988: Cohen weighs whether to roll out teams at scale.

To sum it up: on the surface this looks like a problem with the warehouse picking process, but underneath it touches on the leader's mindset, employees' motivation and the organisation's culture.

  1. Accountability: someone has to own the responsibility

The biggest keyword is Accountability.

In the traditional model, an order is split among different workers, and when something goes wrong, nobody is responsible. The logic of a self-managed team is: one team owns it from start to finish — they earn money together and take the blame together when things go wrong.

But there's a precondition: the leader must be willing to let go.

If the boss watches every detail like a hawk, employees will always be mere "executors" and never "owners."

  1. High Commitment Practices: getting employees to think like owners

In class we heard an example: Walmart gives store managers company stock, so that they care about both their store's results and the company's overall performance.

This is what High Commitment Practices are: turning "my job" into "my enterprise."

In the warehouse, when workers know:

how many cases I picked today → I can work out my pay the same day;

if something goes wrong → the team bears it together;

their sense of identity changes.

Pride in ownership is the strongest motivator there is.

  1. The original problems: low efficiency, high turnover, no accountability

Before C&S introduced the reform, the warehouse was riddled with problems:

Employees were overloaded and exhausted, and accidents were frequent.

Picking aisles were blocked, pallets were left everywhere, and stress was high.

Turnover was extremely high — most of the workforce was replaced within a year.

No one owned responsibility → low motivation, work felt like going through the motions.

This is very much like the pain points of many companies: busy, chaotic, error-prone, and in the end nobody is happy.

  1. Self-managed teams: benefits and risks

Benefits:

Employees have a sense of ownership and are more willing to improve the process.

KPIs are tied to incentives and goals are clear (e.g. 9,000 cases per shift).

Owning an order "from start to finish" → quality improves markedly.

Fast feedback → every day you know how much you earned.

A little story from class:

In 1992, in a small American town, three 17-year-old boys were the most efficient loaders in town. A job that originally took them 4 hours took only 2 once the father designed an incentive of "US$14 per person to load a truck of watermelons."

Incentives and accountability really can squeeze out people's potential.

Risks:

Not everyone is suited to team self-management.

It's easy to start and hard to sustain; leadership has to keep cheering people on.

The team needs the right people: driven, but not the kind who fight for credit and glory.

Training new people also has to be counted in the team's costs, or it will affect output.

  1. Key points for organisational change

The top sets the direction; execution is left to the front line: Planning top-down, Execution bottom-up.

Fast feedback: daily numbers so employees can feel cause and effect.

Culture + compensation, two wheels driving together: without culture to support it, even the best incentives get distorted.

Attract the right people: find people with the right amount of ego — who want to win, but don't just want the credit.

Bring the key opinion leaders (Influencers) along: organisational change has to win these people over first.

Everyone longs to be proud: if they can't find that pride with you, they'll look for it somewhere else.

  1. My Reflections

The professor reminded us to be careful about our assumptions about "human nature." How employees perform is not entirely "human nature"; it's shaped by the organisational environment.

A lot of the time, employees aren't "unmotivated" because they're lazy, but because the organisation hasn't given them room.

Design the right mechanisms, and people's potential goes far beyond what we imagine.

This class reminded me of a line:

"Everyone wants to be proud of something. If the company doesn't give them the chance, they'll find it elsewhere."

At its core, the self-managed team isn't just an experiment in a warehouse; it's a management philosophy:

Give employees room

Build accountability

Drive with feedback

Push forward on the two wheels of culture and incentives

This doesn't just apply to warehouse pickers — it applies to our startup teams too.
