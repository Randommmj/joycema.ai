Strategy Case

How should Alphabet do diversification?

September 21, 2025 · 4 min read

Case Summary

In 2015, Google split itself into its core businesses (Search, Ads, YouTube, Android) and a bunch of exploratory subsidiaries (self-driving cars, smart home, a longevity lab, smart cities...), all gathered under the name Alphabet. What we discussed in this class was: why do this? What benefits and headaches does a structure like this bring? Why: Google's founders believed that to stay innovative, they should be like Berkshire Hathaway, using a holding company structure to house different subsidiaries and keep big-company bureaucracy from slowing down venture-style exploration. Financials: In 2015 Alphabet's total revenue was $74.9 billion, of which Google contributed $74.5 billion (about 99%); Other Bets brought in only $448 million (0.6%) but lost $3.6 billion. Typical examples: Nest (smart home) grew only modestly after being acquired — 2015 revenue was $340 million, far below expectations — and went through turmoil over management problems. Boston Dynamics was sold because it was too far from profitability. The relative successes were Android, YouTube and DoubleClick. Culture: Google was once famous for 20% time and for rewarding failure, but as it grew it became bureaucratic and lost talent; many employees preferred to go start their own companies. Investor reaction: In the early days of the restructuring the share price rose 6.2%. Investors felt transparency had improved and that Ruth Porat (CFO) brought financial discipline. The Q4 2016 results showed strong growth in the Google segment, while Other Bets kept burning cash. The tension: Should Alphabet keep backing the "Other Bets", or concentrate on the core advertising business? Can the holding structure really make innovation succeed, or will it lead to fragmentation and inefficiency? Questions for the future: How can Other Bets actually produce profitable businesses? In the wave of generative AI (Gen AI), should Alphabet change its structure to gain a competitive edge?

Class Notes

  1. The logic of diversification: why "do many things at once"?

When a company diversifies, it has to answer two questions:

  1. Better-off test: if the two businesses sit inside one company, do they create more value than they would independently?

Example: Disney owns films, theme parks and TV networks, so one IP can be monetised again and again across different platforms — that is "supply-side logic".

Google/Alphabet's logic is different: it has billions of users around the world, and this "demand pool" can be developed over and over, driving more new businesses — that is "demand-side logic".

  1. Ownership test: does it have to be owned outright? Or can it be achieved through investment or partnership?

Some businesses you have to do yourself, such as the core search algorithm;

others can be done through investment or partnership, such as a venture fund investing in AI start-ups.

  1. Explore vs. Exploit

Once a company matures, it runs into a dilemma:

Exploit: push the existing core business as far as it can go (Google's ads and search are the "cash cow").

Explore: go after new opportunities that may bring in no revenue for ten years (such as self-driving Waymo or health tech Verily).

The problem is that these two kinds of business need completely different ways of being managed:

Exploit emphasises efficiency, profit and stability;

Explore emphasises trial and error, risk-taking and tolerance of failure.

So Alphabet's approach is to manage the core business and the exploratory businesses separately, giving each the soil that suits it.

Explore vs. Exploit

Recognising the differences here is really important! 👇

Dimension | Explore (innovation) | Exploit (extraction) Strategic intent: innovation, growth | cost, profit Critical tasks: new products, breakthrough innovation | efficiency, incremental innovation Structure: adaptive, loose, team-based, often independent | formal, hierarchical, specialised Processes: focused on generating, selecting and developing ideas; looser financial and operational controls | focused on strategic/operational planning and capital allocation Rewards: milestones, growth | margins, productivity Culture: risk-taking, flexible, experimental | efficiency, quality, customer satisfaction

  1. What are the benefits and headaches of this structure?

Benefits:

A clear separation of where the money comes from and where it goes (investors feel more reassured).

New businesses get room to experiment quickly like start-ups, without being slowed down by big-company bureaucracy.

The founders hoped Alphabet would become "the next Berkshire Hathaway", holding all kinds of companies for the long term.

Headaches:

Money burns fast. In 2015 Other Bets made up less than 1% of revenue but lost US$3.6 billion.

Independent subsidiaries sometimes clash with the parent company's culture (Nest is the classic case: management conflict, and in the end the founder left).

Independence also means knowledge-sharing is hard, and new technology doesn't necessarily flow smoothly into the core business.

  1. Lessons for Arcus42

  2. A company has to learn to "walk on two legs": squeeze every bit of value out of the core business while continually placing bets on the future.

  3. But seeds can't burn cash forever — set milestones and be ready to cut them at any time.

  4. Should exploration sit inside the company? If it sits outside (say, only through investing), you may lose the long-term vision and the capacity to absorb the technology; but if it sits inside, you have to accept the cash burn and the failures.

  5. Not every exploration needs to be owned. Sometimes a market transaction is cheaper; the key is to work out the costs and the strategic value clearly.

  6. Most important of all: a mature company only avoids being left behind when the next technology wave arrives if it keeps its ability to "explore" (Nokia is the cautionary tale).
