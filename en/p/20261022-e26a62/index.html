Finance: Nantucket Nectars

Valuation starts with asking who the buyer is

October 22, 2026

ClassWed, Oct 14 09:00–10:30 (Boston time) · Finance · Martin Sinozich · Prep materials

Unit 3, Professor Martin Sinozich's Finance — The Trade Sale Process, class discussion of Nantucket Nectars: The Exit.

Before class, I focused on whether Nantucket Nectars should sell, and to whom. In class, the professor laid out the financial statements and played recordings of the two founders looking back. The question gradually became more specific: does a company's value belong to the company itself, or to the buyer who happens to need it right now?

Here goes — enjoy:

Growth first has to get past cash flow

Class opened with the two founders' passion. It's easy to see passion as a strength: they went years without drawing a salary, kept putting money back into the company, insisted on product quality, and were willing to do the selling and stock the shelves themselves.

But passion has another side. Insisting on quality can squeeze margins; and if a company depends too much on its founders, buyers will worry about whether the team can keep growing after they leave. A founder's passion can light the fire, but it can't substitute for operating capability that can be handed over.

At this point the professor brought the discussion back to the statements. Revenue was growing, but cash was tight. Inventory tied up roughly two months, and receivables grew along with sales. Not all of the products the company had sold had yet turned into money collected.

One classmate suggested the company could keep raising money and sell later, once profits and distribution were built up. Others pointed out that financing isn't free, and the management team might not deliver on its growth projections. The professor's judgement came down to two things: whether the growth story holds up, and whether the cash can last long enough to deliver on it.

The growth on the income statement may be consuming the cash on the balance sheet. For a growing business, inventory and receivables aren't accounting details; they're the everyday constraints that decide how much longer it can keep running.

Valuation models set the anchor; competition sets the price

The case gave two very different valuation scenarios: about US$34 million to US$58 million as a standalone business; about US$106 million to US$176 million once a strategic buyer's growth, procurement and distribution synergies are added. The wide gap in between is the value a buyer might create.

The professor rebuilt a discounted cash flow model. Raise the discount rate and the valuation falls; change the assumptions on growth, margins or working capital and the result moves too. The class also compared precedent acquisitions and public company valuations, but didn't treat any one multiple as the right answer.

A DCF helps the seller know what they're negotiating about; only bidding makes the buyer say how much they're willing to pay.

The distinction matters. When several companies come asking at the same time, whether you can line them up into an orderly competition is a separate thing from the valuation formula itself. The model helps set the floor and lets the seller know what a lowball offer means; the competitive process gives different buyers' synergies a chance to make it into the price.

The class also discussed the role of investment banks. A bank doesn't just produce a valuation table; it can find the right buyers, maintain confidentiality and run the process, so founders don't have to run the company while personally saying the price things that damage relationships.

This time, Nantucket Nectars' founders didn't hire a bank. They were faced with an unexpected rush of buyers, and with help from lawyers they kept the competition going themselves, one phone call after another. The professor reminded us that this kind of starting point is rare. Many companies never get seven buyers calling them unprompted.

The value a buyer brings also brings integration costs

In class, we went back and forth comparing Ocean Spray, Pepsi and Triarc. Pepsi had the strongest distribution and scale, and in theory could open up the most markets; Ocean Spray could provide fruit sourcing capability, and also needed single-serve distribution and a young brand; Triarc knew the beverage business well, but already owned brands like Snapple, and integrating distribution could damage Nantucket's existing relationships.

The more synergy a buyer can create, the more the target may be worth; but how that synergy is realised also determines what the founders will lose.

What the two founders worried about wasn't some abstract "big-company culture," but very concrete things: whether the product would be reformulated for margin, whether existing distributors would be replaced, whether employees and the brand story would be merged into an unfamiliar system.

In the recording played in class, the founders said they ultimately chose Ocean Spray because it understood the juice business and genuinely understood product quality. They felt Pepsi wouldn't push the price high enough, while Triarc would make employees feel they were betraying the original culture. In the end, Ocean Spray promised to let Nantucket operate relatively independently.

Retained equity needs an exit written into the contract

Ocean Spray bought 80% at a company valuation of about US$80 million; the two Toms together kept 20%, and Egan exited entirely. The founders kept running the business, taking some cash while keeping the possibility of future upside.

But a minority stake doesn't equal control. A few years later, Ocean Spray's management changed, and the new leadership decided to sell the business. The founder recalled that, relying on a five-year clause in the original agreement, he negotiated with the new management over the price of the retained stake, and rejected the compromise they proposed. The class transcript doesn't spell out all the details of the later deal; what is certain is that the deal relationship had changed, and the contract became one of the few bargaining chips left in the founders' hands.

This gave me a new understanding of the pre-class discussion about the "second bite of the apple." Keeping a minority stake lets you keep participating in the future; but without a clear pricing mechanism, sale conditions and dispute resolution, it can also become a piece of paper you can't cash. Your future options have to be written into the contract before the deal.

Deal timing is where buyer demand meets seller readiness

The session ended by returning to a simple fact: buyers can suddenly need a business because the market shifts, and they can also redo the math on a deal because management changes.

Ocean Spray at the time needed distribution capability and a young brand. Several strategic buyers were also chasing New Age beverages. Later both the industry and the company changed, and cultural promises couldn't stop the business from changing hands again.

There's luck in timing, but preparation can make luck more useful. A seller needs to know how long the cash will last, whether shareholders can agree, where the business's standalone value lies, and also think clearly about whether they want to stay on. Only then, when a buyer knocks, can they negotiate price, structure, and life after leaving.

My own take is that selling a business is like answering two different questions: how much the market is willing to pay for it; and what the founder is willing to give up for that price. No valuation model can answer the second one for you.

After Class

Before class, I leaned towards the two Toms selling to Ocean Spray through a competitive process and keeping a minority stake. The actual deal discussed in class was close to that judgement, and its outcome filled in a part the pre-class model hadn't finished: the five-year clause in the contract later became the founders' basis for negotiating with the new buyer.

Companies keep moving forward, and the people who own them change too. So what you keep when you sell isn't just a percentage of equity, but also what you'll still be able to say and do when the future changes.
