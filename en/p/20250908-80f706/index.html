Danaher: How to Squeeze the Value Out of a Mature Business

September 8, 2025

Another company I could never learn to copy, but through it I saw the rules and inevitabilities of how businesses develop and how the business world works.

From 1986 to 2010, over 24 years, Danaher grew revenue from US$296 million to US$13.2 billion, an annualized growth rate of nearly 30%, with annualized shareholder returns of 25%, beating GE and Berkshire.

It's a bit like a PE firm, yet in many ways it goes further than PE firms ever do.

The most important point: just as every person must eventually die, you have to think about the company's entire life cycle!

Corperate Stategy is about when you enter? when you expand and when you exit.

Case Summary

Origins and growth: Danaher was founded by the Rales brothers in the 1980s, initially building itself by acquiring low-profile industrial companies. By the mid-1980s it had already entered the Fortune 500, emphasizing a "No. 1 or No. 2" market position. Culture and methodology: from 1988 it learned from Toyota's lean production, gradually forming the Danaher Business System (DBS), whose core is "continuous improvement (kaizen)." Leadership and transformation: under George Sherman from 1990 to 2001, the company shifted from tires and tools toward higher-value-added areas such as environmental testing and electronic instruments; in 2001 Larry Culp took over as CEO and kept strengthening the platform strategy. Strategic growth platforms: the company is organized around 5 major segments: Environmental, Test & Measurement, Dental, Life Sciences & Diagnostics, and Industrial Technologies. Each platform expands through a model of "large platform acquisitions + small bolt-on deals." DBS in detail: built around four Ps — People, Plan, Process, Performance — with emphasis on a 100-day strategic plan, kaizen events, and Policy Deployment (something like a "lighthouse for strategy execution"). After an acquisition, it rapidly introduces DBS culture and tools. Example: Radiometer, a blood-gas analyzer company acquired in 2005: after the acquisition, DBS cut the production cycle from 18 days to 2, shortened the R&D cycle by 33%, and raised operating margin by 4%, while R&D investment increased at the same time. Results: from 1986 to 2010 revenue grew from US$296 million to US$13.2 billion, an annualized growth rate of nearly 30%; annualized shareholder returns of 25%, beating GE and Berkshire. Challenges: 1. Can growth be sustained when acquisitions slow? 2. Competition from private equity funds (Blackstone and others) is driving up acquisition prices; 3. Can the culture of continuous improvement be maintained over the long term? 4. Is DBS suited to moving into high-tech fields?

Class Notes 1. After an acquisition, how do you get the team "pulling in the same direction"? I can force my children to do things, or by embrace them. If you can't even force your own children, how can you get a team to buy in willingly? How do you help them understand? Asking Questions: co-creating the strategy

Within 100 days of a deal, Danaher works with the target company's management to draw up a "strategic blueprint."

Not by handing them a thick PPT deck and telling them to follow it, but by putting the problems found during due diligence on the table and asking: "Where do you think the gaps are? How do we close them?"

Radiometer's CEO once said: "Danaher didn't tell us what to do; it kept asking: are you really capturing every growth opportunity?"

Through "co-creation," the original management feels it has a voice, and the culture is not steamrolled but activated.

  1. Visible victories (Quick Wins)

Soon after a deal, they run a value-stream mapping + kaizen exercise. For example, Radiometer's plastic-parts process: 18 days → 2 days.

This kind of "see it, touch it" improvement instantly wins over the skeptics: "Oh, so this method really can help me."

Danaher calls this "action learning" (learning by doing).

👉 Culture isn't built by shouting slogans, but by building confidence one small win at a time.

  1. The different stages of a business: Danaher shows up at a company's mature stage. The mature stage is when you should raise efficiency, and DPS happens to raise efficiency — so it isn't like PE, which merely buys; it buys companies in order to "squeeze out the profits."

  2. What do you do when a business has already entered decline? Sell; Change, jump to another technology/industry; Concentrate: like Lego, they jump to provide for the adults instead of children
