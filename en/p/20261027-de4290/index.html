Unit 3: Professor Das Narayandas teaches Market-Facing Functions. The final session: a summary of the unit's concepts, with Starbucks Korea as the optional reading.

Over this unit I wrote pre-class pieces on five of Das's cases: Olay, Belden, Bajaj Finance, Equitable, and Starbucks Korea. Each one stopped at a multiple-choice question.

In the last class he didn't teach a new case. Instead he pulled three weeks of material into a single thread:

Find your own market. Staple yourself to the customer's journey. Make a promise, then have the whole organization — people, processes, AI and compensation — keep it together.

In the final few minutes, he talked about himself.

After class, I wrote one line in my notebook:

Put a purpose in your business, make promises and keep them.

This piece follows the order of the class: what Das said, what I jotted down in class, and what I thought of on my own.

Here goes — enjoy:

Whoever you promote is who your organization becomes

The classroom we met in is what HBS calls the Big Room.

Das opened by explaining what the room is used for: about a hundred tenured professors gather here to decide who gets tenure and who doesn't.

The review report is written by a committee of three tenured professors. The committee spends four months interviewing dozens of peers and experts in the field, and writes a report of about twenty pages — something like a case. Everyone reads it, discusses it in this room for ninety minutes, and then votes: yes, no, or abstain.

He asked: which one is the worst?

Abstain. Four months of preparation and ninety minutes of discussion, and you still can't decide — either the case itself is flawed, or something hasn't been said out loud.

In a professional services firm, the most important decision is whom to promote. Whoever you promote is who your organization becomes. Put the wrong person in this room and they will slowly take apart the institution's character.

When someone isn't promoted, the mood in the room is far heavier than when someone is. Getting to review takes about nine years. Saying no means admitting that eight to ten years of investment have come to nothing. So the decision can't be driven by emotion; it has to be calm and objective, and it can't stray from your own culture.

This was the setup for the day's last topic: good talent only gets more expensive. At the end of the day, he talked about compensation.

Don't say you're small — go find your biggest market

The summary began with MAM — My Addressable Market.

Never say you're a small player in a big pond. Saying that isn't modesty; it means you haven't understood yourself.

When someone in your organization says "we're not big enough," ask them to show you which market we are the biggest in. Then go fight there. Be the 900-pound gorilla in that space: you set the rules, and the game is played your way.

He said that, compared with some of the other people he has taught, what the people in this room lack is resources. So you have to get the maximum return from every resource in your hands. He doesn't buy the venture-capital line of "go global, go fast."

Win on your own turf first, and you earn the right to make your turf bigger.

Don't tell me what you do — tell me what your customers go through

To win on your own turf, you have to make every customer a hero. How? The same line he's repeated all unit: staple yourself to the customer's journey.

The phrase comes from a 1992 HBR article, Staple Yourself to an Order, written by several of his colleagues. Back then, what you stapled yourself to was an order; today, it's the whole journey — from the moment the customer first feels the pain, to consumption, to everything after consumption.

He told the story of Intuit.

Co-founder Scott Cook watched his wife manage the household accounts. She used a checkbook, writing out checks one by one and recording each entry — a real hassle. This was the dot-matrix printer era, and Intuit's first software was a small program to help households write checks.

Later they discovered that many buyers of this home software were small businesses. That made no sense: running a business takes a general ledger and financial statements. At first they assumed users had filled in the registration forms wrong.

Only when they stapled themselves to these small business owners' daily routines did they see it: the accounting software on the market had been written by accountants, for accountants. Small business owners had good ideas and plenty of passion, but they didn't come from business backgrounds, let alone accounting. Asked whether he understood the general ledger, one owner replied: which World War II general was he?

If you don't staple yourself to the customer's journey, you'll miss opportunities that no one else can see either.

Insight isn't only about new products. Friction in the processes customers go through to deal with you is also opportunity. Break your business down into individual processes and see clearly where the people are and where the machines are. Friction mostly comes from manual steps; automating them doesn't mean cutting people — it frees them to do other things.

Then he made a request: from today on, when we meet again, he doesn't want to know what business we're in. He only wants to hear two things — what our customers are going through, and how they experience us.

Before class, I wrote in my Bajaj Finance piece:

Customer-centricity means the customer can walk into the next product on their own, rather than waiting for a team they can't reach to push it.

This class moved that line one step earlier: before the customer can walk in on their own, someone first has to walk the whole path with them.

A brand is a promise; making it and keeping it are two jobs

Das recast Keller's brand pyramid in his own words: a brand is a promise.

First look at what you promised; then at how the customer responds; finally at what kind of relationship they want with you — an emotional embrace, or an efficient business handshake. Different businesses, different answers.

A leader therefore has two jobs: one is to take part personally in making the promise; the other is to make sure their people keep the promises already made.

He also put in a fair word for sales. He's done sales himself and likes it; he just doesn't want us to treat sales as the tip of the spear. A promise has to be kept by the whole organization; sales is only one link. Always putting sales on the highest pedestal tells everyone else in the organization, again and again: you're not good enough. That isn't true.

When he got to the two customer interfaces, the "bow tie" and the "diamond," he made a point of correcting us: the diamond isn't a sales team, it's a customer-promise team — mobilizing the whole company's resources to deliver the experience promised to the customer.

This approach is more expensive. So don't do it defensively; only do it where revenue can grow faster than cost. Perhaps twenty or thirty percent of the business is still better done one transaction at a time.

Being customer-centric doesn't mean being obsessed with every customer.

Saying no to an order isn't saying no to the customer. An order you know you can't do well will just become your reputation. Promises need boundaries — you wouldn't ask a neurosurgeon to look at your toe.

Before class, I wrote in my post-class notes on Olay:

The promise can change; looking down on customers can't stay.

This class filled in the other half: you can make a high promise, but it has to have boundaries.

A new uniform doesn't make you a new role

From a box-selling supplier, to a value-added partner, to a strategic advisor — whether B2B, B2C or professional services, the climb is hard. A new uniform and a few new talking points won't turn you into a solutions provider. You may need different people.

He posed a question. Three account managers: one holds the bottom tier, selling standard products and competing on price and performance; one works at the top as a strategic advisor; one is in the middle, doing a bit of everything. The customer says only one can stay to manage the whole relationship. Whom do you keep?

The bottom-tier one matters most — he's the one earning today's money. But when he looks up at the top tier, he thinks: too far, too complicated, I have to go out hunting every day. He can protect today, but he can't build tomorrow.

The top-tier one loves long dinners and talking about the industry five years out; when the grunt work comes, he feels it's someone else's job. The middle one isn't good at either end, which is why he's in the middle.

He described an experiment he'd been part of. A diversified financial services company assigned an account manager to each of its top hundred clients — a third from the bottom tier, a third from the middle, a third from the top. Two years later they looked at sales, and only one variable explained the results: where the manager originally came from. Wherever they came from, that's the category they sold more of. Cross-divisional, "one firm" selling didn't happen even once — and the compensation had in fact already been adjusted.

If there's one person in the organization willing to step out of their comfort zone, it's you. Don't count on anyone else.

Many colleagues like to say you should turn every employee into an entrepreneur; he disagrees. If someone has the same risk appetite as you, they should start their own company — or walk in and tell you: no base salary, give me 35% commission.

In professional services, the problem is harder. Top-tier clients listen to only two kinds of people: those with gray hair, or rocket scientists. McKinsey relies on gray hair — having seen a lot. BCG took another path and called itself the rocket scientists: the experience curve and the growth-share matrix both came from there. The middle tier relies on deep industry knowledge.

Many professional services firms complain that clients don't respect them. Das said that's because you've never built credibility at that level. Making promises isn't enough; you have to keep them.

Before class, I wrote in my Belden piece that beneath the three hard problems lay one question:

How can a channel system designed to sell parts make room for selling solutions?

This class gave a colder answer: the system has to change, and the people may not be the same people anymore.

Build steps to the moon, and draw the guardrails in plain sight

Coming back to Equitable, he said he doesn't sell any particular methodology. For an organization to keep innovating, it needs five things:

  • Goals: everyone's goals feed into the company's goals
  • Voice of the customer: put out a minimum viable product first, and keep the customer in the room
  • Guardrails: drawn by the leader, and drawn in plain sight
  • Agility: not just the IT department's job — the whole company iterates quickly
  • Skills: look at skills, not titles

Lots of people talk about moonshots. Das said your job is to build the organization a staircase to the moon. Goals can stretch people, but don't break them.

Don't write moonshot goals into basic performance reviews and pay. Give a realistic goal first, then ask the person: by your own reckoning, what's your moonshot? If they hit it, reward them publicly.

Guardrails were what he talked about most. Some things can be declared "not open for discussion" — say a family business says "we manage the finances ourselves" — and that's perfectly fine, as long as it's said clearly and applies to everyone equally. A week earlier, in this same room, he and his colleagues had discussed how to bring AI into HBS teaching, and they spent the first fifteen minutes drawing the guardrails: anything outside the guardrails, they don't do.

Inside the guardrails, shorten the distance between decision and action. He has seen plenty of bosses with a stack of documents on their desk, flipping page after page without reading, signing at the bottom.

That's also why he had us read Starbucks Korea: seven people had signed off before that incident. Each one signed assuming someone else had read it.

One more signature doesn't mean one more layer of management.

The last item is skills. Don't tell him you have three VPs and four AVPs; tell him what skills you have on hand. He admitted this one is the biggest threat to HBS itself — an institution that has always been proud of its titles.

My class notes

Take the moonshot, but build the steps to the moon.

Whatever you do, set goals. Stretch people, but don't break them. Set realistic goals, ask them, let them take the moonshot — and reward them in PUBLIC!! People love to be recognized.

Align people with the org's goal. Discuss the guardrails (you have to!).

My Thinking

Counting skills, not titles — that's the future of organizations.

One step further: if a skill can be done by AI, there should be an AI officer responsible for that part.

Take us as an example: much of the work of a CFO and HR can be done by AI. Take reviewing people: administrative and cashier arrangements still need humans, but analysis and feedback can be done entirely by AI.

— Provided, that is, we have the data.

Before class, I wrote in my Starbucks Korea and Equitable pieces, respectively:

Make reviews correspond to real responsibility. Everyone knows what they're judging, and knows whom to go to when they spot a question. Adding signatures can't replace that.

Who qualifies for the 100% must be spelled out clearly.

Looking back now, those two lines say the same thing: draw the guardrails in plain sight, and put responsibility on specific people.

Sixty percent human judgment, thirty percent machine prediction, ten percent left to the machine

Next he talked about AI, then compensation. Before AI, he made a point of saying: this is a businessperson's take, not a technical one.

He divided a leader's decisions into three types, and left us homework for next year:

  • Descriptive: the machine provides historical data, and humans decide. About 60%
  • Predictive: the machine predicts outcomes, and humans decide. About 30%
  • Prescriptive: humans set the rules, and the machine decides on its own. At least 10%

Descriptive: stop making decisions in Excel

We all grew up in Excel. He put it bluntly: if you're still making decisions in Excel two years from now, you've fallen behind. Use AI, check it for hallucinations, but use it.

The real skill is visualization. Watch how your people spot patterns — the next generation reads charts differently from us. Don't compete with AI; collaborate with it, and let it lay out the patterns in a way everyone can understand. When uncertainty is high and the world is changing too fast, you still have to rely on intuition.

Prescriptive: once the machine decides, stop second-guessing it

His example was his wife's Tesla. He sits in that car and never touches the steering wheel — his wife won't let him. So what's left for the person? Giving direction. Humans set the objective function: maximize profit today, or minimize inventory. The machine executes.

What suits the machine are decisions that are tiny individually, but where each one isn't quite precise enough. Over many repetitions the gap becomes large — dynamic pricing on store shelves, for example.

He has a new HBR article: many companies have just wrapped their existing organization in a shell of AI, and then measure only usage — how many tokens were consumed. An employee asking AI where to go for dinner also counts as usage.

Usage is not adoption.

Predictive: don't fall in love with accuracy

When previous OPM participants showed him their dashboards, nine out of ten featured customer churn. Churn is a lagging indicator — the customer has already left. A better metric is "customers at risk of churning": when a customer who accounts for 20% of revenue starts flashing red, everyone mobilizes to save them.

Data scientists pride themselves on prediction accuracy, and the easiest way to raise accuracy is to strip out the fuzzy gray zones. But a businessperson sees two kinds of cost: false positives bring wasted effort, false negatives bring missed opportunities.

Don't fall in love with accuracy; keep your eyes on wasted effort and missed opportunities. Models should be built by the business people and the data people together.

My class notes

Descriptive analytics: the machine provides historical data. Learn to use AI analysis instead of Excel — Excel is linear.

Once the machine makes a decision, do not second-guess it. Execute.

False positive: the machine says do it, but you shouldn't — wasted effort.

False negative: the machine says don't, but you should — missed opportunity.

Leaving money on the table: the model should not chase 100% accuracy. Take out the outliers, and you will be just normal.

My Thinking

We should let AI run processes. We should also pay attention to where AI has surprised us, and where AI is helping us make decisions.

AI isn't just processes and scheduling; it also makes decisions. An AI-native organization relies not only on AI's automation, but also on AI's judgment.

In my own work, AI can help me assess and screen clients, and help me follow up with them.

At the same time, keep AI in check.

When AI steps onstage, go back to the start of the journey

Das said this is the other reason he had us read Starbucks Korea: AI is moving from backstage to onstage.

Think of a business as a performance. Descriptive and predictive analytics are both backstage; if something goes wrong, there's still time to catch it. Dynamic pricing is already at the edge of the curtain. Generative AI and agents stand directly in front of the customer: writing content for you, talking to customers on your behalf.

Onstage, a single small mistake by AI can destroy all the credibility you've built up in the market. Onstage AI needs someone watching it 24 hours a day. The letting go we talked about earlier shouldn't be applied to decisions that directly affect customer relationships — there, you don't get a second chance.

A classmate asked: as more and more of our customer interactions are with AI agents, how do we keep our promises?

Das's answer: go earlier in the journey. Be with the real customer before they hand their instructions to an AI agent, closer to the trigger point.

When he was in sales, he used to tell his team: if we can't get ourselves written into the customer's requirements spec, this deal is over. That's even truer today. Write yourself in while human emotion is still part of the decision. Once the decision is handed to an agent, the agent uses cold, rational evaluation, and we may no longer have a chance.

Staple yourself to the customer's journey, not the AI agent's.

My class notes

Left side (backstage): allow for what we just discussed.

Right side (onstage): you will not get a second chance at a first impression.

How to keep our promises: the agent has to be taught by the customer. Get closer to the people, go through the customer's journey. Human emotions still need to be involved.

If the machine makes the decision, don't make the user take the blame

When he got to prescriptive analytics, he first told a story, and said he'd come back to it at the end.

When UPS launched its routing algorithm ORION, it piloted it with drivers first. The drivers said: it's unbelievably good — it only arrived yesterday, and it knows my route better than anyone.

Das added: have you ever tried telling a London cabbie he took the wrong route? He'll ask you to get out. When people that proud say an algorithm is better than they are, they mean it.

But after the full rollout, adoption didn't take off. Most drivers were overriding the algorithm's routes.

The problem wasn't the algorithm. If the algorithm succeeds, who gets the credit? Not the driver. If the algorithm fails, who gets punished? The driver. If I'm going to carry the blame when things go wrong, I'd rather keep my own hands on the wheel. So they quietly used the system "with the brakes on."

According to Das, what UPS did next was simple: it stopped measuring on-time rates and customer satisfaction, and looked at just one thing — how often you followed the machine. Late? Fine. Something went wrong? Fine. Adoption happened almost overnight.

People are afraid of being held responsible for someone else's mistakes, even when that "someone else" is a machine.

After covering AI stepping onstage, he came back to this story and placed it on a chart from his HBR article: every decision raises two questions at once — who makes the decision, and who is accountable for the outcome. The UPS drivers landed in the most troublesome box: the machine decides, the human takes the blame.

This isn't a new problem. ERP was supposed to let headquarters see the whole organization, but plant managers were still evaluated on their own plant's performance, so the production schedules sent down from headquarters went straight into the trash. Ask for good data, and people will give you numbers that make it look good.

When the machine decides and the machine is accountable, it's like walking a dog: if the dog relieves itself on the sidewalk, the owner cleans up. Machines make mistakes, so each machine needs a designated owner — the people who designed it, who hold the keys to update it. They clean up, not the people who use it.

Everyone loves to say "human in the loop." He said, don't make the user the human — make the designer the human.

He mentioned Amazon CEO Andy Jassy, who had visited the school a few weeks earlier: there are a few numbers on the dashboard that even he can't touch; he can only tell the people who own the system: we missed, something went wrong somewhere, go fix it. Das told this to the CEO of a consumer goods company, who said it was impossible — the whole reason I exist is to turn these knobs every day.

There's another box that's the reverse: the human makes the decision but blames the machine — the dog ate my homework. Last week he met a group of Wall Street traders complaining that their quant models were inaccurate. When they went back to check, it turned out they had been overriding the models themselves all along.

My class notes

AI can inform you to make better decisions. Become more fluent with AI, so it gives you better visualization to make decisions.

Machines make mistakes. But if you still blame people — watching people's necks — you will be stuck.

Make the designers the human in the loop, or the human on the loop for the rules and regulations.

Before class, I left a question at the end of my Starbucks Korea piece:

When AI makes output faster and cheaper, where are we going to put human attention?

This class gave a concrete answer: on the rules and the exceptions, and on who cleans up after something goes wrong.

You set the rules, and the organization will game them

The last topic was compensation. There are only two clean approaches: pay for behavior, or pay for outcomes.

Pay for behavior, or pay for outcomes

Paying for behavior: the example is the person behind the McDonald's counter. You don't hold them responsible for store traffic; they only need to do two things well: smile, and when a customer buys a burger, ask whether they'd like fries.

There are two preconditions: you know clearly which behaviors lead to results, and you can see those behaviors — hence the camera above the counter. People paid this way look after both the company and the customer, and think long-term. They say: "Let's do this together."

Paying for outcomes is for when you can't articulate the causal link between effort and results — you can only measure the results, so you pay commission on results. People paid this way look after themselves first and value the short term. They say: "I delivered the results; leave me alone."

Two axes, four boxes

The horizontal axis: can you measure effort? The vertical axis: can you link effort to outcomes?

  • Both high: high base, low commission — pay for behavior
  • Both low: pay for outcomes
  • You know what works, but can't see what people are doing: train first, then pay for outcomes
  • You can see the effort, but don't yet know which actions work: learn first, then pay for behavior

In the third box, most companies use a hybrid — a bit of base, a bit of commission. Das said that's not a good idea.

Manage invisible effort with training first, then motivate it with pay. Build your own sales methodology, and no one goes to the front line until they've passed the training. First teach them how to fish and how to bait the hook, then let them live by their skill.

Many companies are afraid people will leave once they're trained, so they don't train. But as long as they're still working for you, one trained person beats an untrained team running around out there.

The fourth box is common in the digital world, where every action is recorded. The task here is learning: use AI and the data you have to learn how customer behavior is changing, and once you've figured it out, move boxes.

When you think about compensation, always think about control at the same time. He calls this the two Cs: control and compensation.

One page, five questions

He has little patience for complicated compensation plans: the more the consultants charge, the more complicated the plan; the more complicated the plan, the harder it is to execute. A compensation system is best explained on a single page.

He recommended five questions, which he said a colleague taught him (the handout credits Ian Larkin):

  1. What good behaviors will this system produce?
  2. What bad behaviors will it produce?
  3. Do the good far outweigh the bad?
  4. How can you reduce the bad — through culture, through training, for example?
  5. How much does reducing them cost, and is it worth it?

On the second question, he repeated a line from the previous two units: your job is to design the rules of the game, and your people will game the rules. Never assume you're smarter than your organization.

He closed with three assumptions about people: people take care of themselves first; their risk appetite differs from yours; and they're always calculating which takes less effort — going along with you or working against you.

My class notes

Build your company's methodology. Don't only focus on compensation — also focus on control!!

Do not let your salespeople go before you train them!!!! Have the most intensive training; do not let them train themselves. Start with training, then move to outcome-based compensation.

Digital world: it's easy to measure. The world has changed. START BY LEARNING, then move to behavior-based compensation.

Always focus on minimizing the effort people need to understand their compensation!!!

"Design the rules of the game, and your people will game the rules!! Do not ever operate under the assumption that you are smarter than others."

People will first take care of themselves. They will always measure the cost.

My Thinking

We need to think through how to design incentives for sales — not only for external sales partners, but for our internal sales team too.

Before class, I wrote in my Belden piece:

People follow the compensation plan, not the strategy speech.

This class completed the second half: people follow the pay, and they also follow the loopholes you didn't design for. So list the bad behaviors as carefully as you list the good ones.

To whom much is given, much is expected

When he finished, he said this was the first time he had taught the whole unit in this version. He'll teach it once more in the spring, then write the book.

In the remaining few minutes, he said he wanted to move his relationship with us from "yelling at you, being tough on you" to something more like friends. He talked about himself.

He was smart from a young age; no exam could stump him. Things came too easily, and that brought other things with it: a touch of arrogance, and no sense of purpose. He always assumed that everything he got was simply his due.

Then his elder son was born. It was the first adversity in his life that he couldn't handle. It was his wife who carried everything, searching everywhere for help for their child. Within a year, they decided to leave India to find a better environment for their son to grow up in.

Going abroad for a PhD wasn't because he wanted to be a scholar; it was the family of three's way out. That's why he calls himself an "accidental academic." A marketing professor who did his PhD in a windowless room and never met a single customer. When he graduated, people suggested he go to a research university; they chose Massachusetts, because it was the best place for their child to grow up.

In his early years in the US, his only thought was getting tenure, to give his family a sense of security. For that, his wife raised the children almost single-handedly.

He said he has made many mistakes. If he could do it over, the one thing he'd most want to change is this: to be a more present father, and a more present partner. He is learning to do that now.

Finally he said: life is hard. You are all very fortunate; life has given you a lot.

Much is expected from those to whom much is given.

To whom much is given, much is expected.

He said his two sons have taught him a great deal.

After Class

What I most want to remember from this class:

Think through and design every part of the business: know where you stand, and know what value you bring to others.

Knowing where you stand is MAM — the space where you write the rules. Knowing what value you bring to others is the promise — what the customer goes through, and how they experience you.

Each of my five pre-class pieces for this unit stopped at its own multiple-choice question. This wrap-up class put them back on a single map: the customer's journey, the promise made, the customer-facing interface, AI's decision rights, and how incentives work.

The brand makes the promise, but it takes processes, decision rights and compensation together to keep it.

Put a purpose in your business, make promises and keep them.

After the spring, Das will turn this course into a book. Our cohort, you could say, got to hear it before it was written.
