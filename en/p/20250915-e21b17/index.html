The "social contract," as seen from Singapore

September 15, 2025 · 4 min read

Case Overview. Starting point: when Singapore became independent in 1965, its GDP per capita was just US$516; it had no resources and a divided society. Yet under the governance of Lee Kuan Yew, GDP per capita reached US$82,808 by 2022. Institutional foundations: public housing (HDB), the Central Provident Fund (CPF), a highly efficient civil service, and the Economic Development Board (EDB) as the "visible hand." It attracted foreign investment (MNCs) on a large scale and drove export-oriented industrialization. State capital: it founded state-owned enterprises (GLCs) such as Singapore Airlines, DBS and the port company, and later set up Temasek and the Government of Singapore Investment Corporation (GIC) to manage its wealth. Economic path: first labor-intensive, then high-skill, then tech innovation. Industry moved from electronics assembly to high-tech manufacturing, biomedicine (Biopolis), aerospace and chips. In services, Singapore became a financial center and a shipping and logistics hub. Social governance: a strong emphasis on multiracial harmony, but a heavy political hand (the PAP has governed for decades), with limits on the press and on assembly. Its social policies earned it the label "Nanny State" — banning chewing gum, restricting smoking in public, and so on. Crisis management: from the 1997 Asian financial crisis to the 2020 COVID pandemic, the government responded quickly. During the pandemic, migrant workers exposed problems of inequality. Current challenges: slowing growth (2023 GDP forecast at 3.5%). An aging population and low fertility (total fertility rate ~1). Reliance on foreign workers vs. local sentiment about jobs. The need to shift from efficiency-driven to innovation-driven growth. The risk of global deglobalization. A moment of handover: Lee Hsien Loong is preparing to pass the baton to Lawrence Wong. Wong has proposed to "refresh the social compact" — keeping growth going while becoming more equal, inclusive and green.

Class Notes

Throughout history, there have always been "empires" dominating the world order. The question is: by 2038, will we see an alliance of small countries, or a set of concentric spheres around big empires?

👉 If we turn the world upside down — suppose the big powers are no longer the protagonists — can small countries become "safe harbors" of stability and innovation?

Singapore's Situation and Choices Back Then

When Singapore became independent in 1965, it was practically "an island with no resources and no hinterland, caught in the middle of the Cold War." It had to solve three fundamental problems:

  1. Politics:

Lee Kuan Yew (LKY)'s top priorities were security and order.

The social contract: the people gave up some freedoms (such as the press and assembly) in exchange for guaranteed housing, jobs and public safety.

A strong bureaucracy (a central bank, a clean and well-paid civil service, continuity of the rule of law) kept the machinery of state running efficiently.

  1. Economy:

"Free market vs. socialism"? The answer was a hybrid.

State-led government-linked companies (GLCs), plus direct investment attracted from multinationals (MNCs).

Streamlined administration and slashed red tape meant foreign firms like Texas Instruments could start production within 50 days.

Guaranteed monetary and institutional credibility attracted long-term capital.

  1. Strategic positioning:

It positioned itself as a "safe harbor" and a "neutral intermediary."

Using its geographic location and trade networks, it turned a small country into a key node in global supply chains.

The Singapore Model's "Social Contract"

The old model: the state trains you to become efficient, technically skilled talent; you hand over part of your freedom in exchange for social security and stability.

The result: decades of high growth that took Singapore "from Third World to First."

But today, that contract is wavering.

The Challenges It Faces Now

  1. The innovation dilemma

Singapore's economic model is built around "efficiency," but efficiency is not the same as innovation.

Creativity, risk-taking and critical thinking are exactly what the old system suppressed.

The experiences of Taiwan and China also remind us: strong execution ≠ strong innovation.

  1. Social tension

Of a population of 5.9 million, about one-third are foreigners.

The number of foreigners has risen since the pandemic, sparking debate about jobs and social fairness.

Young people feel the "social contract has broken down," and their sense of loyalty is declining.

  1. Cost of living

Rising home prices and living costs leave young people feeling that "hard work doesn't necessarily pay off."

The social contract — "as long as you work hard → you'll have a home and security" — is no longer as solid.

  1. The ebb of globalization

The Singapore model depends heavily on globalization: free trade, cross-border capital, foreign talent.

As globalization slows or even reverses, the "efficiency-driven" model starts to hit its ceiling.

Why Transformation Is Inevitable

Singapore has to think about:

From efficiency to innovation: not just "skilled workers," but cultivating entrepreneurs and risk-takers.

From content to context: not just mastering knowledge (content), but having soft skills too (EQ, contextual thinking).

From "growth above all" to "a balanced social contract": fairness, inclusion and green become the new keywords.

If this is no longer the age of globalization, the old model may not work.

Singapore's success rested on "social contract + efficiency + globalization."

But when the environment changes, the contract has to be renewed, or it will lose loyalty and trust.

"In the past, the state fed the family (EAT your FAMILY); in the future, it must rely on the innovative power of families and individuals to give back to the state."

🤔 So...

If the future really is a world of "clusters of small countries," can the Singapore model be replicated?

What clauses should "Social Contract 2.0" contain to still hold up in a post-globalization era?
