Pop Mart: How to Industrialize "Surprise" — From a Small Beijing Shop to the Global Labubu Phenomenon

Opening Hook: An Underrated Beginning

In 2010, in a corner of the Europlaza Mall in Beijing's Zhongguancun, a small stationery and knick-knack shop of barely twenty or thirty square meters opened its doors. The owner was 23-year-old Wang Ning, a young man from Henan who had just graduated in advertising from Sias International College of Zhengzhou University.

He had neither the halo of an Ivy League school nor any connections in venture capital. His four years at university were ordinary; like most of his classmates, his initial goal was simply to find a stable job. But after graduating, unwilling to settle into a life of following the script, he resolutely turned down an offer from an advertising agency and set out on the entrepreneurial path with RMB 100,000 in savings scraped together by himself and his family.

In the shop's first few months, he guarded the shelves every day, watching monthly revenue in the ledger come in at less than US$4,300 (about US$4,100), and often fell deep into thought. The only goal he could come up with was: "Keep the shop alive first."

No one could have imagined that fifteen years later, this little stationery shop would grow into a designer-toy giant with a market capitalization of about US$49 billion, surpassing countless well-known internet companies on the capital markets.

Chapter 1: Three Rounds of Trial and Error — From Selling Goods to Searching for a Soul

First Attempt: Trendy Stationery

Wang Ning initially positioned the shop as a "trendy stationery store," with shelves full of imported Korean stationery. He even went to Hong Kong himself to bring back brands not available on the mainland, hoping to attract students and white-collar workers.

But the stationery business was fiercely competitive with razor-thin margins. He often worked late into the night sorting inventory, only to open the ledger the next day and find that after selling a whole pile of stationery, the profit wasn't even enough to cover utilities and rent.

Second Attempt: Imported Toy Collection Store

In 2013, he decisively pivoted, turning the shop into a "trendy toy collection store" selling Japanese capsule toys, American figurines and little designer dolls from Hong Kong. For a while, the shop really was buzzing, with customers lining up to drop coins into the capsule machines.

But the good times didn't last: these products depended on imports, with long supply cycles and high costs. He often stayed up late running to the airport to pick up shipments, hauling back boxes of toys only to find competitors immediately selling the same items at lower prices. His business was stuck in a loop of "sell a batch, run out, go find more stock." His margins were squeezed almost to nothing.

Detail of failure: At his lowest point, he had to cut prices to clear stock, hanging a "buy two, get one free" banner in the shop, yet customers kept dwindling. During that period he nearly gave up, and even considered going back to find a job.

Third Attempt: Searching for "Emotional Resonance"

Amid the failures, Wang Ning gradually realized: what customers really wanted wasn't a functional "notebook" or "doll," but something that could spark emotional resonance.

In 2015, at the Hong Kong International Toys Fair, he came across Kenny Wong's "Molly." That little girl with lake-blue hair and a haughty look made him instantly understand that "emotion is value."

In 2016, Pop Mart launched its "blind box + IP" model. The randomness of the blind box created "surprise," while Molly's expression satisfied young people's craving for "buying things to make yourself happy." Pop Mart finally moved from "selling goods" into a new stage of "managing emotions."

Chapter 2: Blind Boxes and Emotion — The Secret of the Model

Blind Boxes: Probability as Design

Every box hides an unknown design, and "secret editions" and "limited editions" set users' hearts racing. Blind boxes aren't gambling, but "probability-based experience design." Core users' annual repurchase rate exceeds 80%, with each buying an average of 12 blind boxes a year.

Emotion: Products as Social Currency

Unboxing blind boxes gradually became a social talking point. Consumers weren't just buying a doll, but "the feeling of being understood" — a social identity.

Chapter 3: Labubu's Breakout — The Second Growth Curve

In 2019, Pop Mart partnered with Belgian illustrator Kasing Lung to bring the picture-book character Labubu to market. With its sharp teeth, at once goofy-cute and rebellious, it struck many people as "weird" at first. Yet the younger generation saw themselves projected in its weirdness. Labubu quickly broke into the mainstream, becoming a vehicle for the "loneliness economy" and "self-expression." Business takeoff: In the first half of 2025, Pop Mart's total revenue was about US$1.94 billion; The Monsters (including Labubu) contributed about US$670 million, up 668% year on year. The plush versions sold US$860 million in six months, 44.2% of total revenue. Gross margin reached a whopping 70.3%. Cultural moments: In Los Angeles, fans spontaneously held a "Labubu Rave," treating it as an emotional symbol of the club scene. At the US Open in New York, Naomi Osaka took to the court with a custom Labubu, setting global social media alight. At Beijing's Yongle Auction, a 131cm Labubu sold for about US$150,000, officially entering the realm of fine art.

Labubu became the "second curve" after Molly, powering Pop Mart's new growth engine.

Chapter 4: The Smart Moves Behind It

Testing the Waters with RoboShop Vending Machines

Before opening a store, Pop Mart often deploys RoboShops first as market tests. Three days to install, real-time data collection, and only once a trade area's potential is confirmed does it invest heavily. In the first half of 2025, RoboShop revenue doubled year on year, and "home delivery" was added.

Omnichannel Touchpoint Density

In 2024, Pop Mart had 401 stores and 2,300 RoboShops in China; and 120 stores and 172 RoboShops overseas. Overseas TikTok revenue grew +5,779.8% year on year.

In 2025, stores in the Americas increased to 41, with online revenue up +1,977% year on year.

Compliance as Experience

China requires a ban on sales to children under 8 and mandatory disclosure of odds and value. Wang Ning didn't dodge this, but built the rules into the product: semi-blind/open boxes, guaranteed pulls for members. Compliance became part of the user experience too.

High Share of Proprietary IP

In the first half of 2025, about 99% of revenue came from proprietary IP.

Wearable IP

Labubu was turned into bag charms and accessories, becoming a "social symbol" carried everywhere.

Chapter 5: Capital Markets and Uncertainty. Record market cap: In August 2025, Pop Mart's market capitalization was about US$49 billion. Hang Seng inclusion and share price dip: On September 8 it was added to the Hang Seng Index, but its shares fell 7–9% that same day as investors worried about dependence on a single IP. At a board meeting, Wang Ning said: "Write the heat into the moat." His strategy: a multi-IP portfolio, global expansion, and compliance innovation.

Lessons and Discussion

A Non-Elite Entrepreneur's Breakthrough

From an ordinary university, with no resources, he still succeeded. The key lay in a keen insight into "emotional needs."

The Power of Model Innovation

Pop Mart has no technological moat, yet it restructured the industry's value chain through "IP + blind box + social."

Sticking with Long-Termism

In an era where traffic is king, Wang Ning refused low-price promotions and stuck to the long-term experience. In the end, he won the trust of the world.

Conclusion

The Labubu story is a case of how an ordinary university graduate turned "surprise" into an organizational capability.

From a little shop with monthly revenue of less than US$4,300 to a global IP contributing US$670 million in six months.

Pop Mart proves that in the age of consumption, precisely capturing users' emotional gaps, combined with relentless execution, can let an ordinary entrepreneur build a business empire of his own.
