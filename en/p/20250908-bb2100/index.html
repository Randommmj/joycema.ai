First of all, this professor is one I absolutely adore — probably also one of the professors our whole HBS cohort agrees is among the very best:

Das Narayandas

One of the most respected professors at Harvard Business School (HBS), and head of Executive Education. His research covers B2B marketing, customer relationship management, brand strategy and more. He often brings his research into the classroom, combining theory with real cases. His classes constantly make you step into other people's shoes, coming at a problem from the angles of customers, managers and shareholders — he's "a teacher who can make complicated problems simple."

Every one of Das's classes gets me thinking hard:

  • How do you become a company that creates unique value?

  • How do you find the courage to be the most expensive product?

  • How do you execute perfectly on the ground? Strategy is all very well, but it only counts if you actually do it!

  • How do you close the loop on every tactic?

After every class of his, I come away with a pile of ideas I want to make happen, and I feel grateful to have work to do. I always can't wait to see my team. Case SummaryPest CONTROL vs Pest ILLUMINATIONCompany backgroundBugs Burger Bug Killers (BBBK), headquartered in Miami, had 1985 revenue of US$33 million (equivalent to US$93.2 million in 2023), 600 employees and annual growth of 20%. It had 15,000 customers, mostly hotels, hospitals and restaurants. Founder Alvin “Bugs” Burger was famous for his "zero pests" promise.Industry backgroundThe US had more than 9,000 pest control companies with total revenue of US$2.2 billion, most of them small, family-run and dependent on residential customers. Large rivals included Orkin (US$213 million in sales) and Terminix (US$160 million). Insurance premiums had soared, and service levels across the industry were generally poor.Founder's storyBugs came from a family of exterminators and entered the business after leaving the army; because the industry was rife with corner-cutting and unethical practices, he struck out on his own. He and his wife Sandee started with US$1,200 and zero customers. From early on he made the promise: "100% pest elimination, or your money back."Service guaranteeAn exclusive "Unconditional Guarantee." If pests were found at a customer's site, BBBK would not only refund the fees but also pay any fines, compensate for damage to reputation, and even pay for a year of service from another company. If a restaurant guest saw a cockroach, their meal was free and they got a gift certificate too.Core practicesThe Service Specialists were "Michelangelos," put through 6 months of rigorous training costing US$15,000 per person, well paid, with turnover below 3% (industry average 60%). A service visit took 3–5 hours (rivals: just 30 minutes), and chemicals accounted for 12% of costs (rivals: about 5%). Customers had to sign a sanitation agreement or BBBK would refuse service. A "Red Dot Program" pushed customers on hygiene. Prices were extremely high (4–10 times rivals'): for example, the initial clean-out of Florida's Diplomat hotel cost US$45,000, with a monthly fee of US$7,200.HistoryIn the 1970s it expanded from Florida across the US, winning customers through public relations and a "knightly" image. Clients included five-star hotels and restaurant chains.ChallengesCompetitors copied the "zero pests" slogan. High prices led to customer losses (US$2 million in contracts lost per year). Expanding too fast created management problems. Bugs's own energy, and the succession question. Insurance costs rising sharply.
