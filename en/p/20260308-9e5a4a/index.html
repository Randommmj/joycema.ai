One Mother, Nine Children: A Century of Kennedy Family Glory

March 8, 2026 · 6 min read

A Message for Women's Day

May you wear gentleness as your armor, resilient and full of love,

blooming through the years and enduring through what you pass on.

Today I want to share my guiding light with you — Rose Fitzgerald Kennedy, daughter of a Boston mayor, wife of the US ambassador to Britain, mother of President JFK; a woman of the century who raised nine children and held up America's foremost political dynasty, forging love, discipline and mission into a family cornerstone that has lasted a hundred years.

She is one of the women who has influenced me most, and The Nine of Us, the book written by her daughter Jean, US ambassador to Ireland, completely changed how I understand women's strength.

Rose

The work is from the Women of Kennedy series supported by the Kennedy family, created by the renowned artist Vincent Xeus, and is in the collection of Arcus42's founding family.

In many people's minds, the Kennedys are one of America's most legendary political families.

One president, several senators, and many activists and philanthropists.

For over a century, this family has kept shaping America in politics, business and public service.

But if we turn the clock back, we discover —

the Kennedy family wasn't born into glory;

it began with a mother.

Her name was

Rose Fitzgerald Kennedy.

Over her lifetime she raised nine children.

Nine children, each a pillar

Upbringing is a family's real strength.

Rose regarded raising children as "the most noble profession in the world," and with a replicable set of family education principles, she led each child toward excellence:

Eldest son Joseph Jr.: the family's benchmark, a World War II hero, a role model of courage and responsibility

Second son John: the 35th President of the United States, who embodied the ideals and style of an era;

Third son Robert: US Attorney General, a steadfast champion of civil rights and fairness;

Youngest son Edward: the "Lion of the Senate," one of the most influential senators in American history, serving 47 years;

Daughter Eunice: founded the Special Olympics, rewriting the fate of people with disabilities through love and inclusion;

Daughter Jean: US Ambassador to Ireland, carrying on the family's mission of public service;

The other daughters also worked deeply in charity, society and public causes, becoming extensions of the family spirit.

Rose never saw herself as "the mother of a political family." In her view, her real job was something more long-term: building a family with responsibility, conviction and discipline.

With an exceptional sense of order, firm faith and a quiet, unshowy strength, she became the Kennedy family's anchor.

The starting point: coming from a Boston political family

Rose was born into a family already steeped in public spirit. Her father, John F. Fitzgerald, had been mayor of Boston and was a prominent early Irish-American politician.

From childhood, she lived in an environment where

politics wasn't power, but a public responsibility.

Later, she married Joseph P. Kennedy Sr. — an extremely ambitious businessman and political figure. But many historians believe:

the Kennedy family's spirit and character came not from the father, but from the mother.

Joseph Kennedy was in charge of building wealth;

Rose Kennedy was in charge of building order.

A family of nine children: discipline matters more than talent

Rose's expectations of her children were very clear.

She believed:

success comes from habits, not talent.

At home, there were a few clear rules:

Read every day

Write letters every day

Pay attention to current affairs

Take part in family discussions

In The Nine of Us, Jean also mentions something very interesting:

Rose kept a record card for each child, listing:

Grades

Interests

Health

Friends

Strengths

In a family of nine children, this was almost a household management system.

Someone later remarked:

Rose was really running something like a small organization.

She once said:

"Life is not a matter of milestones, but of moments."

Life isn't defined by achievements; it's made up of experiences.

The "family parliament" at the dinner table

There are so many wonderful details from inside the Kennedy household,

and one of the most famous traditions was

the discussion at the dinner table.

Every day Rose would set out freshly ironed newspapers for the children,

and their father would pose questions at the table, such as:

"If you were president, how would you handle the war in Europe?"

"If the country faced an economic crisis, what would you do?"

"Why would a certain politician make a decision like that?"

Every child had to express their own view.

In Jean's memory, family dinners were almost like a small forum.

Many years later, Robert Kennedy recalled:

our dinner table was our first university.

From a family governance perspective, this was actually a very advanced way of educating children.

What it produced wasn't "obedient children,"

but members with judgment.

When disaster strikes a family, who is its anchor?

Rose's life wasn't only glory.

She went through many family tragedies:

1941: her daughter Rosemary Kennedy was left permanently disabled by surgery

1944: her eldest son Joseph Kennedy Jr. was killed in World War II

1948: her second daughter Kathleen Kennedy died in a plane crash

1963: her second son, President John F. Kennedy, was assassinated

1968: her third son Robert F. Kennedy was assassinated

1969: her husband Joseph died

A mother lost four children.

Fate tested her in the harshest possible way, yet through daily Mass, a strict routine and unceasing charity work, she pieced what was broken back into a whole, and turned pain into legacy.

She wasn't crushed; instead, she showed astonishing resilience. At 69 she learned to drive a car; at 80 she learned to ski like a young person. Her optimism and composure in the face of upheaval became the spiritual pillar that carried the whole extended family through one adversity after another.

She once said something very famous:

"Birds sing after a storm. Why shouldn't people?"

After a storm the birds still sing — so why can't people?

Many historians believe

it was precisely this spirit that let the Kennedy family keep its enormous public influence after tragedy upon tragedy.

This is exactly the core of the "family governance" that we at Arcus42 value — people are a family's core asset; only people can play the crucial role in major decisions and crises, helping a family make it through cycles and change.

A legacy beyond wealth

Rose died in 1995 at the age of 104. Her long life itself made her a living bearer of the family's memory and values; she witnessed the upheavals of the 20th century and passed the family's stories and spirit on to her grandchildren and even great-grandchildren.

In 1951, in recognition of her "exemplary motherhood and charitable works," the Vatican gave Rose the title of "Papal Countess" (Countess of the Holy See); she was the sixth American woman to receive this honor. It was not just a religious honor, but recognition of the social value she had created as a mother and philanthropist.

Looking back on the lives of Rose and her children — through John F. Kennedy's Peace Corps, the Special Olympics founded by Eunice Kennedy, Robert Kennedy's justice reforms and social advocacy, and the whole family's sustained commitment to public service — the Kennedy family's influence long ago went beyond political power and the accumulation of the family's own wealth, becoming a symbol of the pursuit of social progress.

Every member of the Kennedy family has a remarkable story, and I hope to have the chance to write about them at length; they show what "passing on goodness" looks like in concrete form. These brilliant stars of human history are precisely the source of Arcus42's values — passing on the family's spirit and sense of responsibility to future generations, so that family wealth resonates with human well-being.

We believe

"passing on goodness" isn't only charitable giving; it's also actively sharing professional expertise, practical experience and systems thinking, so that every family deserves a better legacy: values, judgment, and an understanding of the future.

Rose's life is the most vivid illustration of "passing on goodness."

At the end of The Nine of Us, Rose's youngest daughter Jean wrote:

"We were lucky children indeed."

We were truly lucky children.

And that luck wasn't only because of wealth.
